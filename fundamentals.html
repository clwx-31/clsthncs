Fundamentals

How training actually works

This is the theory page, and it is the highest-leverage 15 minutes on this site. Everything else — the program, the exercise ladders, the goals — is an application of what's here. If you understand this page, you can fix your own training forever instead of asking the internet.

1 · How your body adapts

Muscle is expensive tissue. Your body will not build or keep it unless you give it a reason. The reason is mechanical tension: muscle fibers being forced to produce high force, repeatedly, close to their limit. Sensors in the muscle detect that tension, trigger a signaling cascade, and — if there is enough protein and enough rest — the fibers repair thicker than before.

Two separate things improve, on two different clocks:

Neural adaptation (weeks 1–8)

Your nervous system gets better at using the muscle you already have: recruiting more motor units, firing them faster, and switching off the antagonist muscles that were fighting you. This is why your push-up count can double in a month with almost no visible change.

Implication: early rep jumps are real strength but not yet size. Don't panic when the mirror lags the logbook by two months.

Hypertrophy (weeks 6 onward)

Actual growth of muscle fibers — more contractile protein laid down inside each cell. Slower, and completely dependent on eating enough and sleeping enough. A realistic ceiling for a well-fed untrained male is roughly 1–2 lb of muscle per month in year one, halving in year two, and halving again after that.

Implication: your first year is the cheapest muscle you will ever buy. Don't waste it eating 1,800 calories.

Connective tissue — tendons, ligaments, joint capsules — adapts on a third, much slower clock: months, not weeks. This mismatch is the single biggest injury risk in calisthenics. Your muscles will be ready for one-arm push-up attempts long before your elbows are. That's why the ladders on this site have rep gates before you move up: the gate isn't about your muscles, it's about buying time for your joints.

2 · Progressive overload — the entire engine

One sentence: if the demand does not increase, the adaptation does not continue. Your body adapts to exactly the stress you give it and then stops. Doing 3 sets of 10 push-ups every session for a year produces a person who is very good at 3 sets of 10 push-ups and otherwise unchanged.

So every session needs a target that beats last time in at least one dimension. In a gym that's trivially "add 5 lb." With bodyweight you have to be more inventive — which is what the next section is about.

The practical test. Open your log. Compare this week's session to the same session three weeks ago. If nothing is measurably harder — more reps, harder variation, slower tempo, less rest, more sets — you are maintaining, not building. Fix it this week.

3 · The five levers: making a bodyweight exercise harder

You can't add plates to your body, but you have five independent dials. Turn one at a time. Turning three at once is how you jump from "too easy" to "can't do it" with nothing in between.

LeverHow it worksExampleUse when
1. Reps & setsMore total work at the same difficulty.3×8 push-ups → 3×12 push-ups → 4×12Always your first move. Cheapest, safest, most measurable.
2. LeverageChange the angle or the distance between your hands and your center of mass, so the same bodyweight produces more torque.Incline push-up (hands on counter) → floor push-up → feet on a chair → pseudo-plancheReps have hit the top of your range (~15–20) and adding more is just cardio.
3. Range of motionLonger range = more work per rep, and more stretch under load, which is a strong growth stimulus.Push-up on the floor → push-up with hands on parallettes so your chest drops below your handsYou want more stimulus without changing the movement you're practicing.
4. Tempo, pauses, and eccentricsMore seconds of tension per rep; pauses kill the elastic bounce; slow lowering (eccentric) is where you are strongest and where most damage-and-repair happens.Normal push-up → 3 seconds down, 1 second pause at the bottom, 1 second upYou have no way to change leverage right now (travel, no equipment) or you're building control for a skill.
5. Stability & limbsTake away a limb or a point of contact. Halving your base of support roughly doubles the demand on what's left, plus adds a big stability tax.Squat → split squat → Bulgarian split squat → shrimp squat → pistol squatThe bilateral version is easy and you have no load to add. This is the answer for legs without equipment.

There is a sixth lever once you own gear: added external load — a weighted backpack, a dip belt, a band. It's the most precise of all, which is why intermediate calisthenics athletes almost all end up using it for the basics while saving leverage work for skills. See Gear, Tier 2.

Rule of thumb for choosing a lever. If you can do more than ~20 clean reps, the exercise is no longer building much strength — change leverage (lever 2) or limbs (lever 5). If you can do fewer than ~5, it's too hard — go back a rung and use reps (lever 1) to build up. The productive band for a beginner is roughly 5–15 clean reps.

4 · Sets, reps, and how much total work

What rep ranges do

The old story — "1–5 for strength, 6–12 for size, 15+ for endurance" — is roughly directionally right but far too rigid. The current evidence says that if you take sets close to failure, anything from about 5 to about 30 reps builds a similar amount of muscle. What changes across the range is what else you get, and how unpleasant it is.

RepsPrimarily buildsNotes for calisthenics
1–5Maximal strength, neural efficiencyWhere skill work lives (one-arm push-up, muscle-up, lever holds). Hard on joints. Not where a beginner should spend most of their time.
6–12Size, with good strength carryoverYour home range. Best balance of stimulus, joint stress, and time. Most of this program lives here.
13–20Size, muscular enduranceFine and useful, especially for legs, core, and early push-ups when you have no way to add difficulty. Sets get genuinely miserable near failure.
20+Endurance; diminishing size returnsUsually a signal you've outgrown the variation. Time to change a lever — not to do 50 push-ups.

How many sets — the volume question

Volume is normally counted in hard sets per muscle group per week (a "hard set" = a working set taken within a few reps of failure; warm-ups don't count).

  • Below ~6 sets/week: maintenance at best. You will not grow much.
  • 10–20 sets/week: the productive zone for nearly everyone. This program lands you around 10–14 per major muscle group, which is right for someone who is new and whose connective tissue is unconditioned.
  • Above ~25 sets/week: returns flatten hard and recovery cost climbs. This is where beginners who read too many forums end up, get exhausted, and blame the program.

Start at the low end. You want the smallest dose that still produces progress, because that leaves you room to add volume later when progress stalls. If you start at 25 sets a week, you have nowhere to go but sideways.

Counting example. In this program, a push-up appears in Session A (4 sets), Session B (3 sets), and Session C (4 sets), plus dips and pike push-ups also hit the triceps and shoulders. So your chest is getting ~11 direct sets a week and your triceps closer to 16 including the indirect work. That is right in the productive zone. This is also why you don't need to add "arm day" — the arms are already working every session.

5 · Proximity to failure — the RIR scale

RIR = Reps In Reserve: how many more clean reps you could have done when you stopped the set. RIR 2 means you stopped with two left in the tank. RIR 0 is true failure.

RIRFeels likeWhen to use it
4–5Comfortable. Bar speed unchanged.Warm-up sets, deload weeks, technique practice, skill work.
2–3Reps are slowing, you're working, but form is clean.Your default. Nearly all of your working sets, especially weeks 1–8. Almost all the growth, a fraction of the fatigue.
0–1The last rep grinds; the next one would break down.Last set of an exercise, occasionally. Isolation and core work where failure is safe. Rep-max testing days.
Beyond failureForm collapses, you cheat the rep out.Basically never as a beginner. It buys nothing extra and costs days of recovery.

Beginners are systematically bad at judging RIR — studies find untrained lifters typically stop 4–6 reps earlier than they think they did. The fix is to occasionally take one set to true failure so you learn what the edge feels like. Once a week, on your last set of one exercise, go until you genuinely cannot complete a clean rep. Then calibrate everything else against that memory.

Never take these to failure: anything overhead where a collapse drops you on your head (handstand push-ups, wall holds), anything where failure means falling from a bar with your body extended (front lever, muscle-up attempts), and skill work generally. Failure is for movements where the worst case is that you slowly sink to the floor.

6 · Tempo and time under tension

Tempo is written as four numbers: eccentric / pause at bottom / concentric / pause at top. So a push-up at 3-1-1-0 means 3 seconds lowering, 1 second paused at the bottom, 1 second pressing up, no pause at the top.

  • Eccentric (lowering) is the phase you're strongest in — you can lower about 1.3× what you can lift. That's the whole reason negatives work as a progression: you can train a pull-up before you can do one.
  • Pauses at the bottom kill the elastic rebound and force the muscle to produce force from a dead stop in a stretched position. Brutally effective and completely free.
  • The concentric should be intentional, not slow. Push with the intent to move fast even when the rep is grinding — that's what recruits high-threshold motor units.

Default tempo for everything in this program: 2-0-1-0. Two seconds down, no bounce, controlled up. If you find yourself bouncing, dropping, or using momentum, you are too high on the ladder — drop a rung.

7 · Rest between sets

Resting too little is the most common self-sabotage in bodyweight training, because sets feel "light" so people rush. Insufficient rest means your next set is limited by breathlessness rather than by muscular fatigue — which means less mechanical tension, which means less growth.

Exercise typeRestWhy
Hard compound work (pull-ups, dips, push-ups, split squats)2–3 minFull recovery of the phosphocreatine system; lets you actually load the muscle on set 3.
Skill / max-strength practice (handstand, levers, negatives)2–4 minSkill degrades with fatigue. Practicing tired teaches you the wrong movement.
Core, calves, accessory60–90 secSmaller muscles, faster recovery, low systemic cost.
Conditioning finisher30–60 secHere the breathlessness is the point.
Superset your rest. A 60-minute session with 3-minute rests sounds impossible until you pair non-competing movements: do a push exercise, rest 90 s, do a pull exercise, rest 90 s, back to push. Each muscle still gets 3+ minutes, but you're never standing around. The program is built this way.

8 · Frequency and why three full-body days wins

After a hard session, muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for roughly 24–48 hours in a trained person, then returns to baseline. If you only train chest once a week, that muscle spends five of seven days not growing. Hitting each muscle 2–3 times per week keeps it elevated more of the time — and the research consistently favors 2+ over 1 when weekly volume is matched.

Three full-body days gives you:

  • Every major pattern trained 3×/week — near-optimal frequency.
  • Lots of practice reps of each movement, which matters enormously when the movements are also skills.
  • Missing one day costs 33%, not 100%. With a 5-day bro split, missing leg day means legs get nothing that week.
  • A full rest day between every session, which is exactly what unconditioned tendons need.

The classic bodybuilding split (chest day, back day, arm day) works fine for people on their fourth year with 6 days to spend. For you, right now, it is strictly worse.

9 · The double-progression rule

This is the single mechanism you'll use to advance for the next two years, so learn it properly. Every exercise gets a rep range and a set count.

  1. Start at the bottom of the range. Example: push-ups, 3 sets of 6, range 6–12.
  2. Each session, try to add reps to any set that isn't at the top of the range. 3×6 → 7/6/6 → 7/7/6 → 7/7/7 → 8/7/7 …
  3. When every set hits the top of the range (3×12) with clean form and 1–2 RIR, you have earned the next rung.
  4. Move to the harder variation and reset to the bottom of the range (e.g. 3×6 feet-elevated push-ups). It will feel like going backwards. It isn't — you are now producing far more force per rep.
  5. Repeat forever.

Two properties make this work. First, it is self-regulating: bad sleep or a stressful week just means you add fewer reps, not that the program breaks. Second, it is unambiguous: you never have to guess whether you're ready to progress. The logbook tells you.

When you stall. Failing to add a rep for two sessions in a row is noise. Three sessions is a stall. In that order, try: (1) sleep and eat more for a week; (2) add one set; (3) take a deload week; (4) drop back one rung and rebuild. Do not just grind the same numbers for six weeks hoping it resolves.

10 · Anatomy of a 60-minute session

BlockTimeWhatWhy in this order
General warm-up5 minRaise body temperature: jog in place, jumping jacks, arm circles, leg swings. Break a light sweat.Warm muscle is more compliant and produces more force; cold tissue tears.

11 · What "good form" actually means

Form advice online is 90% noise. Here is the whole thing in five rules:

  1. Full range of motion. Chest to the floor. Chin over the bar. Hip crease below the knee. Partial reps are the most common way people fake progress. If you can't do full range, the variation is too hard — regress.
  2. Control both directions. If you're falling rather than lowering, you're throwing away the most productive half of every rep.
  3. Brace your midsection. Before each rep: exhale slightly, then tighten your abs as if bracing for a punch, and squeeze your glutes. This locks your ribcage to your pelvis. Half of "bad push-up form" is just an unbraced core letting the hips sag.
  4. Breathe out on the effort. Inhale on the way down, exhale as you press or pull. Don't hold your breath through multiple reps.
  5. Move the joints through paths they like. Elbows around 45° from your torso in a push-up, not flared to 90°. Shoulders pulled down and back before you pull, not shrugged into your ears. Knees tracking over your toes, not collapsing inward.

Everything else — hand width, foot position, exactly where you look — is personal and you will find your own version within a few weeks. The way to check your form without a coach: film one set from the side with your phone every couple of weeks. You will immediately see the sagging hips or the half-range reps that felt fine from the inside.

12 · The six patterns that must all be trained

Programs go wrong when they become a list of favorite exercises instead of a balanced set of patterns. Every week you need all six. A good sanity check for any program: can you assign every exercise in it to one of these rows, and is every row occupied?

PatternTrainsYour movements
Horizontal pushChest, front delts, tricepsPush-up family, dips (partly)
Horizontal pullMid-back, rear delts, bicepsInverted rows, table rows, band rows
Vertical pushShoulders, triceps, upper chestPike push-ups → handstand push-ups
Vertical pullLats, biceps, forearms, gripPull-ups / chin-ups and their regressions
Legs — squat & hingeQuads, glutes, hamstrings, calvesSquat → split squat → pistol; glute bridge → Nordic curl
Core — anti-extension & anti-rotationAbs, obliques, deep stabilizers, hip flexorsDead bug, hollow hold, plank family, leg raises, L-sit

Balance push and pull roughly 1:1, and if anything, favor pull. Almost everyone starts with a strong-ish chest and a weak upper back, then does more push work because it's easier and more visible. The result is rounded shoulders, a forward head, and eventually shoulder pain. Extra rows are the cheapest insurance in all of training.

14 · Glossary

TermMeaning
RepOne complete execution of a movement, start to finish.
SetA group of reps done back to back without rest.
Hard / working setA set taken within ~3 reps of failure. The kind that counts toward volume.
RIRReps In Reserve — how many you left in the tank.
RPERate of Perceived Exertion, 1–10. RPE 8 ≈ RIR 2. Two scales for the same thing.
ConcentricThe shortening/lifting phase — pressing up out of a push-up.
Eccentric / negativeThe lengthening/lowering phase. You're ~30% stronger here, which is why negatives are a progression tool.
IsometricA hold with no movement — plank, L-sit, handstand, dead hang.
Progression / regressionA harder / easier version of the same movement pattern.
VolumeTotal work, usually counted as hard sets per muscle per week.
DeloadA planned easy week to let fatigue dissipate and connective tissue catch up.
Scapular retraction / depressionPulling your shoulder blades together / pulling them down away from your ears. The setup position for nearly every pull.
Hollow bodyRibs down, lower back pressed flat, pelvis tucked. The fundamental calisthenics core position.
Straight-arm strengthForce produced with elbows locked (planche, lever, L-sit). Loads tendons far more than bent-arm work — earn it slowly.
GPPGeneral physical preparedness — baseline conditioning that lets you recover between sets and sessions.
DOMSDelayed Onset Muscle Soreness. Peaks 24–48 h after training. Not a measure of a good workout.
TDEETotal Daily Energy Expenditure — the calories you burn per day, all in.
