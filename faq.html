FAQ & myths

The questions everyone asks in month one

Short, direct answers to the things that generate the most confusion, followed by the myths worth explicitly unlearning and a troubleshooting table for when something isn't working.

Results & expectations

Can you actually build real muscle with just bodyweight?

Yes. Muscle responds to mechanical tension close to failure, and it cannot tell whether that tension comes from a barbell or from your own bodyweight at a bad leverage. Research comparing bodyweight training to weight training with equated effort and proximity to failure finds similar hypertrophy.

Where bodyweight training genuinely struggles is fine-grained loading — you can't add 5 lb, so you have to jump between variations — and legs, where bodyweight stops being challenging quickly. Both are solvable: use the five levers, go single-leg, and add a loaded backpack when you outgrow a variation.

How long until I see results?
  • Week 1–2: soreness, then it fades. Nothing visible.
  • Week 3–4: reps climbing noticeably. That's your nervous system, not new muscle.
  • Week 6–8: you start to notice small changes — usually shoulders, arms, and posture. Nobody else does yet.
  • Week 10–14: other people start commenting. This is the classic "have you been working out?" window.
  • Month 6: obvious to anyone who knew you before.
  • Month 12: a genuinely different body, if you ate and slept for it.
I'm 5'9" and 150 lb. Am I too skinny for this?

You're at an ideal starting point, and specifically a better one than someone heavier. Calisthenics is a strength-to-weight sport: every movement in it is easier at lower bodyweight. A lighter person gets their first pull-up, dip, and handstand far sooner, which means more early wins, which means they don't quit.

Your BMI is 22.1 — squarely healthy. You are not underweight; you are under-muscled, which is a training and eating problem, not a weight problem. Trying to fix it by just getting on a scale-driven bulk without training would only add fat.

Will lifting or calisthenics stunt my growth if I'm a teenager?

No. This is a persistent myth with no support in the evidence. Major sports-medicine bodies — including the American Academy of Pediatrics — endorse resistance training for adolescents, noting improvements in strength, bone density, body composition, and injury resistance. Growth plates are damaged by acute trauma (a fall, a bad landing, a maximal lift with terrible form), not by well-supervised progressive training.

Bodyweight training is a particularly safe entry point because the load is inherently capped at your own weight. Use good form, progress gradually, don't chase 1-rep maxes, and don't train through joint pain — the same advice that applies to adults.

How much does genetics actually matter?

It matters a lot for your ceiling and your rate, and almost not at all for whether this is worth doing. Muscle fiber type, tendon insertions, limb lengths, and hormone levels vary widely, and studies of identical training programs consistently find some people gain three times as much as others.

What genetics does not determine is whether you'll be dramatically stronger and better-built in two years than you are today. Essentially everyone who trains consistently and eats for it gets that. Limb length also cuts both ways in calisthenics: long arms make pull-ups harder but levers easier; short limbs are the reverse.

Training questions

I can't do a single pull-up. What do I do?

Completely normal — most untrained beginners can't. Run all three of these in the same week:

  1. Negatives. Jump or step to the top, lower for 5 seconds. 3 sets of 5. Builds the top half of the movement.
  2. Band- or foot-assisted full reps. Teaches you to pull from a dead hang, which negatives never do.
  3. Rows and scapular pull-ups. Your mid-back initiates the pull, and it's usually the weak link.

Also: start with chin-ups (underhand). They're meaningfully easier because the biceps contribute more, and most people get a chin-up 2–4 weeks before a pull-up. Expect 4–12 weeks to your first rep from a true zero.

Should I train every day? Can I do more than 3 days?

You can eventually, but not now. Three full-body days with rest between them hits every muscle 3×/week — near-optimal frequency — while giving unconditioned tendons the recovery they need. Adding days as a beginner produces more fatigue, not more growth, and it's the most common route to a month-4 injury.

What you can do daily: walk, do the 10-minute mobility routine, practice handstands briefly, and grease the groove with one easy pull-up rep in passing. None of that is "a workout," and none of it costs meaningful recovery.

Is the "100 push-ups a day" type of challenge worth doing?

As a challenge, sure — it builds a habit and it's fun. As a training program, no. Doing the same easy exercise for high reps every day means no progressive overload, no recovery, one movement pattern, and nothing for your back or legs. You end up good at push-ups and imbalanced everywhere else, often with cranky shoulders.

If the daily-challenge format is what gets you to show up, use it for the first month to build the habit, then switch to the actual program.

High reps or low reps for muscle?

Anything from about 5 to about 30 reps builds similar muscle if you take the set close to failure. That's the key qualifier. 6–12 is the practical sweet spot: enough tension to build strength, not so many reps that the set becomes a cardiovascular event.

In calisthenics the real signal is different: if you're over ~20 reps, the exercise has stopped being a strength stimulus and you should change a lever. If you're under 5, it's too hard — regress.

Do I need to feel sore for it to have worked?

No. Soreness reflects novelty and eccentric damage, not effectiveness. You'll be sore in weeks 1–2, sore again whenever you introduce a new movement, and then mostly not sore even while making your best progress. Experienced trainees who are growing well are rarely very sore.

Judge your training by whether the numbers in your log are going up. That's the only reliable indicator.

Should I train if I'm still sore?

Usually yes. Light-to-moderate training on sore muscles typically reduces soreness through increased blood flow, and it doesn't impair recovery. Cut a set or two if you need to.

Exceptions: if the soreness is severe enough to change your form, train a different pattern that day. And if it's sharp, joint-located, or one-sided, it isn't soreness — check the pain traffic light.

How do I build legs without weights?

Go single-leg. Splitting the load onto one leg roughly doubles the demand, and adds a big balance and stabilizer tax on top. The ladder — split squat → Bulgarian split squat → box pistol → shrimp squat → pistol squat — provides years of progression at 150 lb.

Then add the two things everyone forgets: hamstrings (sliding leg curls, Nordic negatives, single-leg RDLs — squats barely touch them) and calves (single-leg raises off a stair, high reps). A loaded backpack extends all of it further. If you eventually want serious leg size, a pair of adjustable dumbbells is the honest answer — nobody gives out prizes for equipment purity.

Can I mix calisthenics with weights or running?

Yes. They aren't opposing religions. Adding dumbbell work for hamstrings, rear delts, and calves fills the exact gaps calisthenics leaves. Running is good for your heart and recovery capacity.

The one caution while bulking: high-volume endurance work burns calories you then have to eat back, and it competes for leg recovery. Keep it to 2–3 easy sessions a week and avoid long runs the day before or after leg-heavy days.

How often should I change my program?

Far less often than you want to. "Muscle confusion" is not a real physiological mechanism — muscles respond to tension and progressive overload, not novelty. Constantly switching exercises means you never get good enough at any of them to load them properly, and it destroys your ability to track progress.

Run this program for a full 24 weeks. The variations change constantly as you climb ladders; the structure shouldn't.

Is it OK to train fasted?

It's fine if you prefer it and your performance holds up. There is no fat-loss or muscle-gain advantage to it, and there's a modest disadvantage: hard sets late in a session tend to suffer without fuel on board. Since you're trying to eat more, not less, training fasted also removes a natural eating opportunity.

If you train early, a banana and a scoop of whey 30 minutes beforehand is enough.

Body composition

How do I get visible abs?

Two independent things, and only one is training. (1) Build the muscle — hollow holds, leg raises, ab wheel, L-sits, and heavy compound work all thicken the abdominal wall. (2) Reduce the body fat covering it — that is entirely a nutrition outcome. Visible abs typically appear around 10–12% body fat for men and 18–20% for women.

There is no such thing as spot reduction: you cannot choose where fat comes off, and crunches don't burn abdominal fat. At 150 lb and lean-ish, you are likely close enough that building the muscle is the higher-leverage half — which is also convenient, because it's the half that's compatible with bulking.

Can I build muscle and lose fat at the same time?

Yes, in specific circumstances, and you happen to be in one of them: complete beginners, people returning after a layoff, and people with higher body fat can all recomposition at maintenance calories with high protein.

That said, at your body composition it's the slower path. You have room to add muscle, so a lean bulk will get you further in the same time. Recomp is the right answer if you're starting at higher body fat.

How do I get the V-taper / wide back look?

Lats and rear delts, plus a waist that doesn't grow much. That means pull-ups above everything else, plus rows, plus keeping the bulk lean so the waist stays put.

Wide-grip pull-ups are not the secret — they shorten the range and stress the shoulder without building a wider back any faster. Full-range pull-ups with progressively more load is the actual answer.

What about my grip? My hands hurt and I'm getting calluses.

Calluses are normal and useful — they're protective. Keep them filed down flat with a pumice stone or file after a shower. Thick raised calluses are what tear off mid-set, which is genuinely painful and costs you a week of hanging.

If grip fails before your back does, add dead hangs at the end of every session, and use liquid chalk. Skip lifting straps for now — grip strength is worth having, and pull-ups build it for free.

Practical & logistics

I live in an apartment. Will this annoy my neighbors?

Calisthenics is close to the quietest strength training there is — no plates, no dropping anything. Two things make noise: jumping (jump squats, jumping jacks, kick-ups landing hard) and a doorway pull-up bar knocking against the frame.

Fixes: swap jumping jacks for marching or step-jacks in the warm-up, land handstand kick-ups softly (which you should be doing anyway for control), train on a mat, and pad the pull-up bar contact points with a folded towel.

What time of day should I train?

The time you'll actually do it, consistently, for months. Strength does peak slightly in the late afternoon for most people, but the effect is small enough to be irrelevant next to the effect of skipping sessions.

Whatever you pick, keep it constant — it makes the habit automatic, and it makes your session-to-session numbers comparable.

Home or park?

Both. A park with a pull-up bar and parallel bars is a free, better gym — bars at proper height, full headroom, and being outside genuinely helps adherence for a lot of people. Home is weather-proof and has zero travel friction.

The best setup: a doorway bar at home as your default, and a park session when the weather's good. Always have an indoor fallback so a rainy week never breaks the habit.

Should I take creatine? Is it safe?

Creatine monohydrate is the most-studied sports supplement in existence, with decades of safety data in healthy people. It reliably improves performance in repeated high-effort sets by a few percent, which compounds over months. 5 g a day, any time, no loading phase needed. Buy plain monohydrate — the "advanced" forms are marketing.

It does cause a couple of pounds of water retention inside the muscle, which is harmless but will show on the scale in week one — don't misread that as fat. If you're under 18, run it past a parent or doctor first; the safety data is mostly in adults, and it is genuinely optional. Fix sleep, calories, protein, and consistency first — those matter 50× more.

Do I need to do cardio?

For muscle, no. For health, recovery capacity, and not being winded between sets, yes — and it's cheap. Two or three easy 25–40 minute sessions a week (brisk walking counts) plus generally walking a lot covers it without eating into your surplus or your recovery.

Skip long runs and high-volume intervals while you're bulking. See the cardio section.

Motivation & mindset

How do I stay motivated?

You don't — motivation is a feeling and feelings are unreliable. Build systems that work when motivation is absent:

  • Fixed days and a fixed time, in your calendar as recurring events. Remove the decision.
  • Lower the activation cost. Mat out, bar up, clothes ready. Most skipped sessions die in the first two minutes.
  • Track everything. Watching numbers climb is the most durable motivator there is, and it's the one that survives a bad mood.
  • Set process goals, not outcome goals. "Three sessions this week" is entirely in your control; "gain 10 lb" is not.
  • Never miss twice. One missed session is noise. Two in a row is how a habit dies.
  • Show up short. On bad days do the 20-minute minimum session. Preserving the streak is worth more than the workout.
I missed three weeks. Did I lose everything?

No. Meaningful muscle loss takes considerably longer than three weeks of inactivity, and what you do lose comes back much faster than it was built — muscle memory is a real, well-documented phenomenon involving retained nuclei in the muscle fibers.

Come back at the same rungs with 2–3 sets instead of 4, expect real soreness for one week, and you'll be back where you were within two to three weeks. The thing you actually lost is habit momentum — so restart today, not on Monday.

I feel self-conscious training in public.

Everyone at a park bar was once unable to do a pull-up, and the ones who are good at it are unusually likely to be encouraging about it. In practice people are almost entirely occupied with themselves.

Practical steps: go at off-peak hours at first, wear headphones, have your session written down so you never look lost, and give it three visits. It stops mattering fast.

Myths worth explicitly unlearning

The mythWhat's actually true
"Bodyweight training only builds endurance, not size."Muscle responds to tension near failure regardless of its source. Use hard variations in the 5–15 rep range and you build size.
"Spot reduction — crunches burn belly fat."Fat is mobilized systemically. You cannot choose where it leaves from. Abs come from building the muscle and lowering overall body fat.
"Toning is a different kind of training.""Toned" means muscle plus low enough body fat to see it. There is no separate toning stimulus — it's the same training.
"Muscle turns into fat if you stop."Different tissues entirely; neither converts to the other. Stopping means muscle atrophies and, if you keep eating the same, fat accumulates. Two processes, not one.
"You must eat protein within 30 minutes of training."The elevated protein-synthesis response lasts many hours. Total daily protein and its distribution across meals are what matter.
"Lifting stunts teenage growth."Not supported by evidence. Properly progressed resistance training is endorsed for adolescents and improves bone density.
"No pain, no gain."Effort, yes. Joint pain, no. Training through the wrong kind of pain converts a two-week annoyance into a six-month problem.
"More sweat means a better workout."Sweat reflects temperature and humidity, not stimulus. A heavy set of pull-ups in a cold room builds more muscle than an hour of sweaty circuits.
"You need to confuse the muscle with constant variety."Muscles respond to progressive tension, not novelty. Constant switching prevents both loading and tracking.
"Rest days are for people who aren't serious."Adaptation happens on rest days. Training without them just accumulates fatigue and injury risk.
"Carbs at night make you fat."Total daily calories determine fat gain. Timing has a negligible effect. A pre-bed protein-and-carb meal is genuinely useful.
"Sit-ups give you a six-pack."They train hip flexors more than abs and don't touch body fat. Hollow holds, leg raises, and ab wheel rollouts are far better ab exercises.
"Stretching before training prevents injury."Static stretching before max effort slightly reduces force output and doesn't prevent injury. Warm up dynamically; stretch afterward or separately.
"You have to go to failure every set."Sets at 1–3 RIR produce nearly identical growth with far less fatigue and injury risk. Failure is a tool, not a default.
"Deep dips and behind-the-neck pulls build more muscle."They mostly build shoulder problems. Range of motion is good up to the point where the joint leaves a safe position.

SymptomMost likely causeFix
Reps not increasing for 3+ sessionsUnder-eating, under-sleeping, or a rung that's too hard to add reps toIn that order: eat more, sleep more, drop a rung and rebuild. Consider a deload.
Weight not going upNot in a surplus, regardless of what you think you ateAdd 250 kcal/day. Recheck the 7-day average in 3 weeks. Repeat until it moves.

Pull-ups stalled while bulkingYou're lifting more bodyweight than you wereExpected. Track reps × bodyweight instead of raw reps.
Wrist pain in push-ups and planksSkipping wrist prep, or too much handstand volume too soonDo the wrist sequence every session; train on fists or parallettes meanwhile.

Still stuck? Work the "it's not working" diagnostic in order. In the overwhelming majority of cases the answer is one of the first three rows: not enough time has passed, not enough food, or nothing in the logbook is going up.
