Where the claims on this site come from

Fitness advice is mostly repeated, rarely sourced. This page lists the research behind the specific numbers used here — volume targets, protein ceilings, sleep, tendon adaptation — plus an honest account of what the evidence doesn't settle, and where this site quietly assumes you're a young adult male.

Read this before you read the list. These are named by author, year, title, and journal so you can find them yourself — search links go to PubMed. Deliberately no DOIs or page numbers, because a citation detail that's slightly wrong is worse than one you had to look up. If a number here matters to a decision you're making, read the actual paper: study populations, training status, and effect sizes matter more than the headline. And nothing here is medical advice.

How to read fitness evidence

Most of what circulates as fitness advice is one of three things: an extrapolation from a mechanism ("X raises Y in a test tube, therefore X builds muscle"), a single small study, or somebody's successful personal routine. All three are weak. A rough hierarchy, strongest first:

  1. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews of randomized trials — pools many studies, so noise averages out. Still only as good as the studies inside it.
  2. Randomized controlled trials in the relevant population. Watch the training status of the subjects: results from untrained beginners often don't transfer to trained lifters, and vice versa.
  3. Observational studies — can show associations, cannot establish cause.
  4. Mechanistic and acute studies — measure a signal (protein synthesis over 4 hours, a hormone spike) that may or may not translate into muscle months later. This is where most supplement marketing lives.
  5. Expert practice and coaching consensus — genuinely useful where research is thin, which is most of skill progression. Label it as what it is.
  6. Anecdote and personal routines — near-zero evidential weight, high entertainment value.

Three habits that will save you from most bad advice:

  • Ask about effect size, not just significance. "Statistically significant" can mean a 1% difference. Many real effects are too small to matter next to consistency.
  • Ask who was studied. Trained vs. untrained, young vs. old, male vs. female, calorie surplus vs. deficit. These change results dramatically.
  • Expect individual variation. Studies of identical programs consistently find some participants gaining several times what others do. Group averages tell you where to start, not what you'll get.

Training: volume, frequency, load, failure

Claim on this sitePrimary source
10–20 hard sets per muscle per week is the productive range; more volume helps up to a point, then flattens. (Fundamentals §4) Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2017), Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Journal of Sports Sciences. find →
Training a muscle 2–3×/week beats 1×/week when weekly volume is equal — the basis for full-body 3-day training. (Fundamentals §8) Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2016), Effects of resistance training frequency on measures of muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Sports Medicine. find →
Roughly 5–30 reps builds similar muscle if sets are taken near failure — which is what makes bodyweight training viable. (Fundamentals §4) Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger (2017), Strength and hypertrophy adaptations between low- versus high-load resistance training: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. find →
Stopping 1–3 reps short of failure produces nearly the same growth with much less fatigue — the basis for the RIR guidance. (Fundamentals §5) Refalo, Helms, Hamilton & Fyfe (2023), Influence of resistance training proximity-to-failure on skeletal muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review with meta-analysis, Sports Medicine. find →
Beginners systematically overestimate how close to failure they are — why the site tells you to occasionally take a set to true failure to calibrate. Steele, Endres, Fisher, Gentil & Giessing (2017), Ability to predict repetitions to momentary failure is not perfectly accurate, though improves with resistance training experience, PeerJ. find →
Longer rest (2–3 min) between hard sets improves strength and hypertrophy compared with 1 minute. (Fundamentals §7) Schoenfeld et al. (2016), Longer interset rest periods enhance muscle strength and hypertrophy in resistance-trained men, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. find →
Early strength gains are largely neural, with measurable size change following later. (Fundamentals §1) Sale (1988), Neural adaptation to resistance training, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise; and Damas et al. (2016), Early resistance training-induced increases in muscle cross-sectional area are concomitant with edema-induced muscle swelling, European Journal of Applied Physiology. find →
Muscle returns faster than it was built after a layoff ("muscle memory"). (FAQ) Gundersen (2016), Muscle memory and a new cellular model for muscle atrophy and hypertrophy, Journal of Experimental Biology; Bruusgaard et al. (2010), Myonuclei acquired by overload exercise precede hypertrophy and are not lost on detraining, PNAS. find →
High-volume endurance training interferes with strength and size gains; moderate amounts largely don't. (Program §cardio) Wilson et al. (2012), Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. find →

Bodyweight vs. weights

This is the claim the whole site rests on, so it's worth being precise. The strong version — "bodyweight training is equivalent to weight training" — is not established. What is reasonably supported:

  • Progressive push-up training produces strength and muscle-thickness gains comparable to bench pressing when load and progression are matched. Kotarsky, Christensen, Miller & Hackney (2018), Effect of progressive calisthenic push-up training on muscle strength and thickness, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. find →
  • Load per se matters much less than proximity to failure (the low- vs. high-load meta-analysis above), which is the mechanism that lets bodyweight work at all.
  • Calisthenics training improves strength, posture, and body composition against non-training controls. Thomas, Bianco, Paoli & Palma (2017), The relation between calisthenics training, posture, strength and body composition, Isokinetics and Exercise Science. Small trial, non-elite population — suggestive rather than decisive. find →

Where bodyweight training is genuinely at a disadvantage, and where the site says so: fine-grained load progression, and lower-body loading past the point where single-leg variations stop being hard. Neither has much dedicated research — that's a mechanical argument, not an empirical one, and it's labelled as such on the start page.

Sleep & recovery

Claim on this sitePrimary source
Sleeping under 8 hours substantially increases injury risk in young athletes — the source of the "roughly doubles" figure. (Recovery §sleep) Milewski et al. (2014), Chronic lack of sleep is associated with increased sports injuries in adolescent athletes, Journal of Pediatric Orthopaedics. find →
Sleep restriction shifts weight loss away from fat and toward lean mass, and worsens body-composition outcomes. Nedeltcheva et al. (2010), Insufficient sleep undermines dietary efforts to reduce adiposity, Annals of Internal Medicine. find →
One week of restricted sleep measurably lowers testosterone in healthy young men. Leproult & Van Cauter (2011), Effect of 1 week of sleep restriction on testosterone levels in young healthy men, JAMA. find →
Cold-water immersion blunts long-term strength and hypertrophy adaptation — why the site says not to ice-bath while building. (Recovery §DOMS) Roberts et al. (2015), Post-exercise cold water immersion attenuates acute anabolic signalling and long-term adaptations in muscle to strength training, Journal of Physiology. find →
Psychological stress impairs recovery from strenuous exercise. (Recovery §stress) Stults-Kolehmainen & Bartholomew (2012), Psychological stress impairs short-term muscular recovery from resistance exercise, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise. find →

Tendons & injury

Claim on this sitePrimary source
Tendon adapts far more slowly than muscle — the reason for conservative rep gates. (Recovery §tendons) Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis (2015), Human tendon adaptation in response to mechanical loading: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Sports Medicine – Open; Magnusson, Langberg & Kjaer (2010), The pathogenesis of tendinopathy in athletes: balancing the response to loading, Nature Reviews Rheumatology. find →
Heavy slow eccentric loading is effective for tendinopathy — the basis of the elbow rehab protocol. (Recovery §injuries) Alfredson et al. (1998), Heavy-load eccentric calf muscle training for the treatment of chronic Achilles tendinosis, American Journal of Sports Medicine; Cook & Purdam (2009), Is tendon pathology a continuum?, British Journal of Sports Medicine. find →
Isometric holds can reduce tendon pain and are useful loading. Rio et al. (2015), Isometric exercise induces analgesia and reduces inhibition in patellar tendinopathy, British Journal of Sports Medicine. find →
Gradual load progression is the main modifiable injury risk factor — sudden spikes in training load predict injury. Gabbett (2016), The training–injury prevention paradox: should athletes be training smarter and harder?, British Journal of Sports Medicine. find →

Training under 18

The claim that resistance training stunts growth has no support in the literature, and the major position statements say so explicitly:

  • Stricker, Faigenbaum & McCambridge / AAP Council on Sports Medicine and Fitness (2020), Resistance training for children and adolescents, Pediatrics. find →
  • Faigenbaum et al. (2009), Youth resistance training: updated position statement paper from the National Strength and Conditioning Association, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. find →
  • Lloyd et al. (2014), Position statement on youth resistance training: the 2014 International Consensus, British Journal of Sports Medicine. find →

Growth-plate injuries in youth resistance training are rare and are associated with unsupervised maximal lifting and poor technique — not with progressive, well-coached training. Bodyweight training is a particularly low-risk entry point because the load is capped at your own weight.

What the evidence does not settle

Plenty of what's on this site is coaching consensus rather than research. It is presented as guidance because it works in practice, but you should know which is which:

On this siteStatus
The specific rep gates on each ladder (3×12 before moving up, etc.)Coaching convention. No study establishes these thresholds. They are deliberately conservative to buy tendon adaptation time.
The exact ordering of progressions within a ladderConsensus and biomechanics. Broadly agreed among coaches; individual leverages change the difficulty order.
Deloading every 6 weeksPractice, weakly supported. Fatigue management is real; the 6-week interval is a convention, not a finding.
"1–2 lb of muscle per month in year one"Practitioner models (Alan Aragon's and Lyle McDonald's rate-of-gain models), widely used, not derived from a single trial. Individual variation is large.
Skill practice done fresh and short of failureMotor-learning theory and gymnastics practice. Sound reasoning, thin sport-specific evidence.
The specific strength standards tableWidely circulated gym benchmarks, not normative population data. Use as a map, not a verdict.
Push:pull volume ratios for shoulder healthClinical reasoning and coaching consensus. Plausible and low-cost; not established by trials.
Optimal handstand teaching progressionsGymnastics coaching tradition. Effective in practice; essentially unstudied.

Who this site assumes you are

Every number here was set for one specific person: a healthy male in his late teens or twenties, 5'9", 150 lb, untrained, uninjured, training at home three days a week. That's useful precision, and it's also a bias worth naming. Here's what changes if that isn't you.

If you're…What stays the sameWhat changes
Female Every exercise, every progression, the program structure, progressive overload, and the training principles. All of it. Calorie and BMR numbers (the calculator handles this), body-fat reference ranges, and the upper-body strength standards. See below.
Over 35 Program structure, exercises, progression logic. Longer warm-ups, slower connective-tissue adaptation, more recovery between hard sessions, and higher protein. See below.
Under 16 Training is safe and beneficial; bodyweight is an ideal entry point. Emphasize technique over load, sleep 8–10 hours, skip supplements including creatine without a doctor's input, and expect that some of your progress is growth rather than training.
Heavier than 150 lb Everything, but the ladders will take longer. Pull-ups and dips are meaningfully harder — you're lifting more. Spend longer on regressions; that's not failure, it's physics. If you're above roughly 20% body fat, a short cut first makes every movement easier.
Carrying an injury Nothing — get an assessment first. This site cannot tell you which movements are safe for your specific injury. A physical therapist can, in one appointment, and it's worth the money.
Already trained The reference material and the ladders above your current rung. The beginner rate-of-gain figures, the 24-week timeline, and the volume ramp are all too conservative for you.

If you're female

Almost nothing about the training changes. Muscle physiology is the same, the hypertrophy response to training is proportionally similar, and women recover well between sets and sessions. What actually differs:

  • Calories are lower for the same height and weight — roughly 160 kcal/day less in the Mifflin–St Jeor equation. Use the calculator with sex set correctly rather than the 2,800 default.
  • Protein target is the same in g/lb — around 1 g per pound of bodyweight.
  • Healthy body-fat ranges are higher. Roughly 21–31% is the normal range; essential fat is around 10–13% versus 2–5% for men. Chasing male-typical numbers is harmful and can disrupt menstrual and bone health.
  • Upper-body strength standards are lower relative to bodyweight — largely a difference in upper-body lean mass distribution. A first pull-up typically takes longer and is a bigger achievement. Lower-body relative strength is much closer to parity, and hamstring and glute work often progresses faster.
  • Iron intake matters more, especially if you menstruate. Low ferritin is a common and easily-missed cause of feeling flat in training.
  • You will not "get bulky" by accident. Gaining meaningful muscle takes years of deliberate effort and eating, for everyone.
  • Training through the menstrual cycle: current evidence does not support programming around cycle phases for most people. Some find strength dips in the days before menstruation — if so, treat it like any other bad-recovery day and reduce volume rather than restructuring the program.

Worth reading: Nunes et al. (2020), Systematic review and meta-analysis of protein intake to support muscle mass and function in healthy adults, and the growing literature on sex differences in resistance training response — Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger (2020), Sex differences in resistance training: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. find →

If you're over 35

  • You still build muscle. Resistance training produces hypertrophy well into the seventies and beyond. The rate is slower, not zero, and the health return is larger.
  • Warm up longer — 10 minutes rather than 5, and never skip the joint prep.
  • Connective tissue adapts more slowly still. Take the rep gates even more seriously; consider adding two weeks at each rung.
  • Protein goes up, to roughly 1.0–1.2 g/lb — older muscle is less sensitive to a given dose of protein ("anabolic resistance"), so per-meal doses matter more.
  • Recovery between hard sessions takes longer. Three days a week is right; a fourth is usually a mistake.
  • Deload more often — every 4–5 weeks rather than 6.
  • Prioritize the movements you'd miss: single-leg strength, grip, and overhead range. Those are the qualities that predict independence later.
