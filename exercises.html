Exercise library

Every movement, arranged as a ladder

Calisthenics has no dumbbell rack, so difficulty comes from ordered progressions instead. Each ladder below runs from "you can do this today, at 150 lb, with no equipment" to genuinely advanced. Each rung has a gate: the exact performance that earns you the next rung.

How to use this page. Every rung carries its own setup, execution, the one cue that matters, and the mistake people actually make on it — open Setup & execution under any rung. You do not do every exercise here. You pick one rung per ladder — whichever one puts you in the 5–15 clean rep range — and that is your exercise until you clear its gate. The rung finder picks that rung for every ladder in about two minutes, and the program tells you which ladders to use on which day. Come back here when you clear a gate, or when something hurts and you need a regression.

Warm-up & joint prep

Five minutes, every session, before anything else. Wrists and shoulders take the most abuse in calisthenics and get the least attention; this is what keeps them healthy.

Wrist prep sequence 2 min · every session

Your wrists are about to hold your entire bodyweight in extension — a position they never otherwise experience. Do all of these on the floor, 10 slow reps or a 20-second hold each:

  1. Palms down, fingers forward. Rock forward and back over your hands, keeping palms glued down.
  2. Palms down, fingers pointing back at your knees. Rock gently backward. This one stretches the forearm flexors hard — go easy.
  3. Backs of hands down, fingers pointing back. Rock gently. Stretches the extensors.
  4. Fists down, knuckles flat. Rock forward and back. Builds the option to train on fists when wrists are cranky.
  5. Fingertip pushes. On all fours, push down through fingertips only, 10 reps.
  6. Wrist circles, 10 each direction, with fingers interlaced.

Why it matters: wrist pain is the #1 reason beginners abandon handstand work. Two minutes a session prevents almost all of it.

Shoulder prep: dislocates, pull-aparts, scapular work 2 min · every session
  • Towel/band dislocates × 10. Hold a towel wide overhead, arms straight, and take it back behind you and return. Go wider if it pinches. Never force it.
  • Band pull-aparts × 15. Arms straight in front holding a light band, pull it apart to chest level, squeezing shoulder blades together. Best single exercise for beginner shoulder health.
  • Scapular push-ups × 10. In a push-up plank with locked elbows, let your chest sink between your shoulder blades, then push the floor away and round your upper back. Only the shoulder blades move.
  • Scapular pulls × 10 (once you have a bar). Hang with straight arms, then pull your shoulder blades down and back to raise your body an inch or two. No elbow bend. This is the missing skill in 90% of failed pull-ups.
  • Arm circles 10 forward, 10 back, big and slow.
Lower body prep: leg swings, deep squat hold, ankle rocks 1–2 min
  • Leg swings 10 front-to-back and 10 side-to-side per leg, holding a wall.
  • Deep squat hold, 30–60 s. Sit in the bottom of a squat, heels down, elbows inside knees pushing them out. If your heels lift, put a book under them and work toward flat.
  • Ankle rocks × 10/side. Half-kneeling, drive your knee forward past your toes keeping the heel down. Ankle mobility is the hidden limiter in pistol squats.
  • Glute bridges × 15 to wake up the glutes before squatting.

Horizontal push — the push-up ladder

Trains: pectorals, front deltoids, triceps, serratus anterior, and (if you brace properly) the entire anterior core. This is your bread and butter for the first year.

  1. Wall push-upGate: 3 × 20Cue Squeeze your glutes so your hips do not lead or trail — the whole body moves as one plank.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Nothing but a wall.

    Stand an arm's length from a wall, hands flat on it at chest height and slightly wider than your shoulders. Step your feet back until your body is a straight line from ears to heels. Bend your elbows to bring your chest to the wall over two seconds, then press back.

    Most common error Standing too close, which turns it into an arm bend with no load.

    First time This will feel too easy, and that is correct. You are teaching your shoulder blades and your midsection to hold one rigid line. Nothing should hurt. If your wrists complain, put your hands on fists instead.

    Too hard? If your hips sag or your shoulders shrug up to your ears, step in closer to the wall and take a set off.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 clean reps with a two-second lower. Then move to a kitchen counter.

  2. High incline push-up (counter)Gate: 3 × 15Cue The higher your hands, the easier it is — use that to dial the difficulty precisely.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A kitchen counter, a windowsill, or a solid desk at roughly hip-to-chest height. Don’t have it? A wall at first, then the back of a heavy couch pushed against a wall. Any fixed surface works — what matters is that it cannot slide.

    Hands on a kitchen counter or a solid desk, slightly wider than your shoulders, feet walked back so your body is a straight diagonal. Lower until your chest touches the edge, elbows tracking about 45° from your torso, then press away.

    Most common error A surface that slides or tips. Push on it hard before you trust it.

    First time Your triceps will tell you about it the next day more than your chest will. That is normal at this angle. Push hard on the surface before you trust it with your weight.

    Too hard? If your chest cannot touch the edge without your hips dropping, use a higher surface. There is no penalty for going higher.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with your chest touching the edge every rep. Then drop to a chair seat.

  3. Low incline push-up (chair)Gate: 3 × 15Cue Push the floor away rather than thinking about lifting yourself — it keeps the shoulder blades from collapsing.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair seat, a stair, or the arm of a couch — roughly 18 inches up. Don’t have it? The second or third step of a staircase gives you every height between here and the floor. It is the most adjustable free equipment you own.

    Hands on a chair seat, a stair, or the arm of a couch, roughly 18 inches up. Same straight line and same 45° elbows; chest touches the surface each rep.

    Most common error Letting the hips sag as the angle gets harder. If they sag, go back to a higher surface.

    First time This is the rung where most people first feel their chest working rather than just their arms. Make sure the chair is against a wall so it cannot slide out from under you.

    Too hard? If the last two reps break form, move up one stair. Half a step is a real jump in difficulty at this height.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with your chest touching the surface. Then either drop a stair or switch to knee push-ups.

  4. Knee push-upGate: 3 × 15Cue Hips fully extended. If you are folded at the hip this becomes a much shorter, much easier rep.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor. A folded towel or a rug under the knees if the floor is hard.

    Kneel with your knees a foot or so behind your hips, hands under your shoulders, and cross your ankles. Push your hips forward until you are a straight line from knees to head, then lower your chest to the floor and press up.

    Most common error Sitting back toward your heels, which shortens the lever and skips most of the work.

    First time Watch for the hip fold — it is the near-universal fault here and it makes the rep about a third shorter. Film one set from the side if you are unsure.

    Too hard? If your hips fold as you tire, go back to a low incline. A folded knee push-up is a worse exercise than a clean incline one.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with hips fully extended and your chest touching the floor. Then start negatives.

  5. Negative push-up (5s down)Gate: 3 × 8Cue Count out loud. The last rep should still take a full five seconds — when it does not, the set is over.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Start at the top of a full push-up on your toes. Lower yourself for a slow five-count until your chest touches the floor. Drop to your knees, press back to the top, get back on your toes, repeat.

    Most common error Collapsing in the last foot. That final stretch is the part building the strength you are missing.

    First time Negatives make you sore. Genuinely sore, for two or three days, the first two times you do them. That is the eccentric loading doing its job and it fades fast. Do not add sets because it felt easy during the session.

    Too hard? If the last rep takes less than three seconds, the set is over. Stop, do not grind out sloppy ones.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 where the last rep still takes a full five seconds. That is your full push-up.

  6. Full push-upGate: 3 × 12Cue Elbows at about 45° from your body — from above your arms and torso make an arrow, not a T.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Hands slightly wider than shoulders and under or just below them, fingers spread and gripping the floor, feet together. Brace your abs and squeeze your glutes into one rigid line. Inhale, lower for two seconds until your sternum touches the floor, exhale and press to a full lockout.

    Most common error Half reps. Chest to the floor or drop back a rung; nobody is impressed by forty partial push-ups.

    First time The one that counts. Sternum to the floor or it is not a rep — and nobody is impressed by forty partial push-ups. Expect your first honest set to be far fewer reps than you assumed.

    Too hard? If your sternum does not reach the floor, or your hips lead, take a set off before you take reps off. Still failing? Go back to negatives for two weeks.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with your sternum touching the floor. Then elevate your feet six inches.

  7. Feet-elevated push-upGate: 3 × 12Cue Keep the ribs pulled down. Elevated feet make the lower back want to arch.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair, step, or couch 12-18 inches high. Don’t have it? Stairs, again — six inches at a time.

    Feet on a chair or step 12–18 inches up, hands on the floor. Everything else is a full push-up. The higher the feet, the more weight goes through your shoulders and upper chest.

    Most common error Going straight to a high box. Add height in small steps — six inches at a time.

    First time More weight goes through your shoulders here. If the front of your shoulder pinches, drop the height and check that your elbows are at 45 degrees, not flared to a T.

    Too hard? If your lower back arches, lower the feet. Ribs pulled down is the requirement, not the suggestion.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 at this height. Then add six inches, or move to diamond push-ups for the triceps.

  8. Diamond push-upGate: 3 × 12Cue Elbows brush past your sides, not out wide — that is what shifts the work onto the triceps.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Hands together directly under your sternum, index fingers and thumbs touching to form a triangle. Lower until your chest touches your hands, elbows staying close to your ribs, then press up.

    Most common error Letting the wrists take a bad angle. Turn the hands out slightly or use fists if they complain.

    First time Your triceps are the limit here, not your chest, so the rep count drops hard from full push-ups. That is expected, not a regression.

    Too hard? If your wrists hurt, turn the hands out slightly or make fists. If your elbows hurt, stop — go back to full push-ups and come back in a month.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with your chest touching your hands. Then move to a deficit.

  9. Deficit push-upGate: 3 × 12Cue The pause at the bottom is the point — it kills the bounce and loads the chest at full stretch.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallettes, push-up handles, or two stacks of books 4-6 inches high. Don’t have it? Two thick hardcover books, or two low stools. They only need to be stable and the same height.

    Hands on parallettes, push-up handles, or two stacks of books 4–6 inches high. Lower until your chest drops below the level of your hands, pause for a beat in that stretched position, and press up.

    Most common error Diving deeper than your shoulders are comfortable with. Add depth gradually.

    First time The pause at the bottom is the exercise. It kills the bounce and loads your chest at full stretch, which is exactly where the growth is — and exactly where it feels hardest.

    Too hard? If your shoulders pinch at the bottom, reduce the height. Add depth over weeks, not in one session.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with a one-second pause at the bottom. Then archer push-ups.

  10. Archer push-upGate: 3 × 8 /sideCue Almost all the weight on the bending arm. The straight arm is a kickstand, not a second pressing arm.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor. Parallettes make the wrist angle kinder.

    Hands wider than a normal push-up. Lower toward your left hand, bending that elbow while the right arm stays straight and slides out to the side, palm rotating so the fingers point away. Press back to centre and alternate.

    Most common error Twisting the hips to cheat the load across. Keep them square to the floor.

    First time The straight arm is a kickstand, not a second pressing arm. Almost all the weight belongs on the bending side, which is why 8 reps here is much harder than 12 full push-ups.

    Too hard? If your hips twist to shift the load, you are not ready. Go back to deficit push-ups, or do these with the straight arm on a low step.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 per side with the hips square. Then pseudo-planche push-ups.

  11. Pseudo-planche push-upGate: 3 × 8Cue The lean is the exercise. If your shoulders drift back over your hands it becomes an ordinary push-up.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor, or parallettes. Full wrist prep is mandatory.

    Hands down at waist level rather than under the shoulders, fingers turned out to the sides. Lean your shoulders forward well past your hands and hold that lean through the whole rep as you lower and press.

    Most common error Doing these with cold wrists. This rung demands the full wrist prep, every session.

    First time The lean is the exercise. If your shoulders drift back over your hands it has quietly become an ordinary push-up. This rung demands the full wrist prep every single session — no exceptions.

    Too hard? If your wrists hurt at all, do these on parallettes or fists. If the lean collapses, shorten the range.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 holding the forward lean the whole rep. Then start one-arm work.

  12. One-arm push-up progressionGate: years away — listed so the ladder does not look finishedCue Resist rotating. The free hand stays behind your back and the hips stay level.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A counter, then a chair, then the floor.

    Elevated one-arm push-ups first (hands on a counter, then a chair), then on the floor with feet very wide, then progressively narrower.

    Most common error Attempting these before archer push-ups are comfortable. The elbow is what pays.

    First time Do not attempt these until archer push-ups are comfortable. The elbow is what pays for impatience here, and elbow tendinopathy takes months to settle.

    Too hard? Raise your hands. A one-arm push-up on a counter is a real exercise and a legitimate place to spend months.

    Too easy? There is no gate here. This is years of work, listed so the ladder does not look finished.

Full push-up — setup, execution, and the five things that go wrong core lift

Setup

  1. Hands slightly wider than shoulder width, directly under or just below your shoulders — not up by your head.
  2. Fingers spread, pointing forward or very slightly out. Grip the floor with your fingertips.
  3. Feet together or a few inches apart. Together is harder (less base), apart is more stable.
  4. Squeeze your glutes and brace your abs until your body is one rigid plank. Tuck your pelvis slightly so your lower back isn't arched.
  5. Push the floor away slightly so your upper back is not collapsed between your shoulder blades — this is called protraction and it protects the shoulder.

Execution

  1. Inhale. Lower under control over ~2 seconds.
  2. Keep elbows at about 45° from your torso — from above, your arms and body make an arrow shape, not a T.
  3. Touch your chest (sternum, not your face) to the floor. Nose an inch off the ground.
  4. Exhale and press up, driving through the whole hand. Push the floor away rather than thinking about lifting yourself.
  5. Lock out fully at the top without letting your hips sag or your shoulders shrug.

What goes wrong

  • Sagging hips / arched lower back. Unbraced core. Squeeze glutes harder and tuck the pelvis; if you can't hold it, regress a rung.
  • Piked hips. Usually a cheat to shorten the range. Straight line, ears to ankles.
  • Flared elbows at 90°. The classic shoulder-impingement setup. Pull them in to 45°.
  • Head leading. Chin reaching for the floor while the chest stays high. Keep your neck neutral — eyes at a spot ~1 ft in front of your hands.
  • Half reps. Chest must reach the floor. If it can't, go back to inclines. Nobody is impressed by 40 half push-ups.

Variations by emphasis

  • Wider hands → more chest, less triceps, more shoulder stress.
  • Narrower / diamond → more triceps and inner chest.
  • Feet elevated → more upper chest and front delts.
  • Hands elevated → more lower chest, easier overall.
  • Hands turned out ~30° → often kinder to cranky wrists and elbows.

Dips — the second pressing pattern

Trains: lower chest, triceps (heavily), front deltoids. Dips are the closest bodyweight equivalent to a heavy press and are worth building toward. They are also the movement most likely to irritate a shoulder, so respect the depth rule below.

  1. Bench dip, knees bentGate: 3 × 15Cue Keep your back close to the chair — drifting forward puts the shoulder in a bad position.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair, bench, step, or bed edge. Don’t have it? The edge of a bathtub, a low wall, or a stair. Anything solid at roughly knee height.

    Sit on the edge of a chair, hands beside your hips gripping the edge, fingers forward. Slide your hips off the front, knees bent and feet flat and close. Lower until your upper arms are parallel to the floor, then press back up.

    Most common error Dropping below parallel because it feels easy. Depth is where dips hurt people.

    First time Bench dips put your shoulders in an internally rotated position that some people simply do not tolerate. If the front of your shoulder pinches, stop and use push-ups and pike push-ups for your triceps instead — this whole ladder is skippable until you have parallel bars.

    Too hard? Bring your feet closer in and take more weight through your legs. Reduce the depth if your shoulders complain.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15. Then straighten your legs.

  2. Bench dip, legs straightGate: 3 × 15Cue Shoulders stay pressed down away from your ears at the bottom.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair, bench, or step. Don’t have it? Same as above.

    Same, with your legs extended and only your heels on the floor. Longer lever, more of your weight on the arms.

    Most common error Shrugging at the bottom, which is the position that irritates shoulders.

    First time Keep your back close to the bench. Drifting forward is what turns shoulder discomfort into shoulder pain here.

    Too hard? Bend your knees again and walk your feet in.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with legs straight and a controlled depth. Then elevate your feet.

  3. Bench dip, feet elevatedGate: 3 × 12Cue Treat this as a stepping stone, not a home. Get to parallel bars.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Two chairs, or a chair and a step. Don’t have it? A bench and a coffee table.

    Heels on a second chair so your body is level between the two. Lower to parallel and press.

    Most common error Living on this rung. The behind-the-back arm position gets less shoulder-friendly the harder it gets.

    First time This is the top of what a chair can give you. Do not chase depth here — go find parallel bars or rings instead, which are both safer and better.

    Too hard? Lower the feet, or go back to legs straight on the floor.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12. Then move to parallel bars if you have access to them.

  4. Parallel-bar support holdGate: 3 × 30 sCue Actively push down through the bars. A passive hang in the support position is what you are training out of.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallel bars, dip bars, gymnastic rings, or two very solid chair backs. Don’t have it? A park has parallel bars. Two kitchen counters facing each other work if they are the right distance apart.

    Jump or step up between parallel bars with your arms locked straight. Push your shoulders down away from your ears, brace your core, point your toes, and hold completely still.

    Most common error Swinging your legs. Stillness is the skill here.

    First time Shoulders down and away from your ears, elbows locked. This hold is what conditions the shoulder and elbow before you start loading them through range — do not skip it to get to real dips faster.

    Too hard? Keep a toe on the ground to take weight off. Hold for less time rather than breaking the shape.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 30 seconds with locked elbows and depressed shoulders. Then negatives.

  5. Negative dip (5s down)Gate: 3 × 5Cue Slight forward lean held constant through the descent.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallel bars or rings, and a step to get to the top. Don’t have it? Jump or step up to the top position from the floor.

    Get to the top support position, then lower for a slow five-count until your upper arms reach parallel. Step off and reset.

    Most common error Letting the shoulders roll forward and up as you tire.

    First time Dips load the shoulder and the sternum harder than almost anything else on this site. Go to about 90 degrees at the elbow, not further, until you have months of them behind you.

    Too hard? If the last rep takes under three seconds, stop. Reduce the depth before reducing the tempo.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5 with a controlled five-second lower. Then full dips.

  6. Parallel-bar dipGate: 3 × 10Cue Stop at parallel. Going deeper feels impressive and is where labrum and biceps-tendon problems come from.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallel bars or rings. Don’t have it? A park dip station.

    From the support hold, lower under control until your upper arms are parallel to the floor, then press back to a full lockout. A slight forward lean emphasises the chest; staying vertical emphasises the triceps.

    Most common error Bouncing out of the bottom. Pause for a beat instead.

    First time Lean forward slightly for more chest, stay upright for more triceps. Stop at 90 degrees at the elbow — going deeper is the most common way people hurt their shoulder on this movement.

    Too hard? Band assistance, or drop back to negatives for the last set.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 10 to 90 degrees. Then add weight or move to rings.

  7. Weighted or ring dipGate: 3 × 8, rings turned out at the topCue On rings, keep them pressed against your body — letting them drift out is how the shoulder gets loaded badly.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Rings, or parallel bars plus a loaded backpack. Don’t have it? A backpack with books. Rings cost about $40 and are the best value on the gear list.

    Add a loaded backpack or dip belt, or move to rings and let the stability demand do the work. On rings, turn the rings out at the top so your palms face forward.

    Most common error Adding weight before you have ten clean bodyweight dips.

    First time Rings move, which is the point and also the difficulty. Expect to shake violently the first few sessions — that is stabiliser recruitment, not a problem.

    Too hard? Take the weight off, or go back to fixed bars. Rings are substantially harder than bars.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 with the rings turned out at the top.

The depth rule. Stop when your upper arms reach parallel with the ground. Going deeper feels impressive and puts the shoulder in maximum extension under load — that is where labrum and biceps-tendon problems come from. Also keep a slight forward lean (chest-focused) or stay vertical (triceps-focused), but do not let your shoulders roll forward and up toward your ears at the bottom.

Vertical push — pike push-up to handstand push-up

Trains: deltoids (all three heads), triceps, upper traps, and the whole overhead stability system. This is how you build shoulders without a barbell, and it doubles as handstand preparation.

  1. Incline pike push-upGate: 3 × 12Cue Hips high. The higher the hips, the more this is a shoulder press and not an incline push-up.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair or a low table. Don’t have it? A bed edge or a couch arm works. Stairs let you fine-tune the height.

    Hands on a chair, walk your feet in and push your hips up high so your torso is as close to vertical as your hamstrings allow. Lower the top of your head toward the seat between your hands, then press back up.

    Most common error Straight legs at the cost of a rounded back — bend the knees instead.

    First time Hips high is the whole game. The higher your hips, the more this is a shoulder press and the less it is an incline push-up.

    Too hard? If your back rounds trying to get your hips high, bend your knees. Rounded back with straight legs is worse than a bent-knee pike every time.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with your head touching down between your hands. Then take it to the floor.

  2. Floor pike push-upGate: 3 × 12Cue Your head goes down between your hands; your chest does not travel forward.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    From a push-up position walk your feet toward your hands into a downward-dog shape. Hands slightly wider than shoulders. Lower the crown of your head to the floor between and slightly in front of your hands, so head and hands form a triangle. Touch lightly, press straight back up.

    Most common error Letting the hips drop, which turns it into a decline push-up and takes the shoulders out of it.

    First time Your head goes down between your hands and your chest does not travel forward — head and hands make a triangle. Expect far fewer reps than a normal push-up; your shoulders are much smaller than your chest.

    Too hard? If your hips drop and your chest travels forward, it has become a decline push-up. Go back to the chair.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with a light head touch. Then elevate your feet.

  3. Feet-elevated pike push-upGate: 3 × 10Cue This is a genuine overhead press — treat the reps with the same respect.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair or couch. Don’t have it? A bed, or the second stair.

    Feet on a chair or couch, hands on the floor, hips stacked high so your torso is close to vertical. Same head-to-triangle path, same lockout.

    Most common error Raising the feet so high that you cannot control the descent to the floor.

    First time This is a genuine overhead press now. Treat the reps with the respect you would give a barbell over your face.

    Too hard? If you cannot control the descent, lower the feet. You should never be dropping the last few inches onto your head.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 10 under control. Then move to wall walks and start the handstand line.

  4. Wall plank / wall walkGate: 60 s holdCue Push tall through your shoulders the whole time, as if trying to make yourself an inch taller.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall and about four feet of floor. Move anything breakable.

    Start in a push-up position with your feet against the base of a wall. Walk your feet up the wall and your hands in toward it, a few inches at a time, until your body is vertical and your chest is close to the wall. Hold, then walk back out under control.

    Most common error Walking your hands in further than you can walk back out from.

    First time Being upside down is disorienting the first few times and your heart rate spikes. That fades within two or three sessions. Learn to walk back out before you go all the way in.

    Too hard? Walk your hands out a few inches. Only ever walk in as far as you can walk back out from.

    Too easy? A 60-second hold close to the wall. Then chest-to-wall handstand holds.

  5. Chest-to-wall handstand holdGate: 60 s totalCue Chest to wall, never back to wall — back-to-wall teaches a banana-shaped arch you then have to unlearn.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall. A folded towel for your head on later rungs.

    Face the wall, hands about a hand-span from it, and walk up into a vertical handstand with your chest and thighs toward the wall. Squeeze glutes, tuck the pelvis, pull the ribs down, push tall.

    Most common error Craning your neck to look forward. Look at the floor between your hands.

    First time Chest to wall, never back to wall — back-to-wall teaches a banana-shaped arch you then spend months unlearning. Look at the floor between your hands, not forward.

    Too hard? Hands further from the wall makes it easier. Shorten the hold before you break the shape.

    Too easy? 60 seconds of total hold time, in any number of sets. Then partial handstand push-ups.

  6. Partial wall handstand push-upGate: 3 × 5Cue Elbows track forward and slightly out, and your head moves toward a spot in front of your hands.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall and a folded towel.

    Kick up with your back to the wall, heels resting on it. Lower a few inches by bending the elbows, then press back to a full lockout. Increase the depth over weeks.

    Most common error Going to failure. Failure here means your head hits the floor — always leave two reps.

    First time Never go to failure here — failure means your head hits the floor. Always leave two reps in the tank. This is the one place on the site where stopping short is a safety rule, not a preference.

    Too hard? Reduce the depth. An inch of honest range beats four inches of collapse.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5, then add depth over weeks until your head touches the towel.

  7. Full wall handstand push-upGate: 3 × 8Cue Head and hands form a triangle, exactly as in the pike push-up.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall and a folded towel.

    Same setup, but lower until the top of your head touches a folded towel on the floor, then press all the way up.

    Most common error A hard head-to-floor contact. Touch the towel, do not rest on it.

    First time Touch the towel, do not rest on it. Head and hands form the same triangle as the pike push-up.

    Too hard? Reduce the depth or add a thicker towel.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 full-range. Then start freestanding work, or add a deficit.

Pike push-up — how to actually do it right core lift

Setup: From a push-up position, walk your feet toward your hands until your hips are as high as possible and your torso is as close to vertical as your hamstrings allow. Hands slightly wider than shoulders. Bend your knees if tight hamstrings force your back to round — you want a vertical torso, not straight legs.

Execution: Lower the top of your head toward the floor between and slightly in front of your hands, so your head and hands form a triangle. Elbows track forward and slightly out — not flared to the sides. Touch lightly. Press straight back up.

Most common error: turning it into a decline push-up by letting the hips drop and pressing forward instead of up. If your chest is going toward the floor rather than your head going down between your hands, your hips are too low.

Progress cue: the higher your feet and the more vertical your torso, the more of your bodyweight your shoulders carry. That is your leverage dial.

Vertical pull — the pull-up ladder

Trains: latissimus dorsi, biceps, brachialis, forearms and grip, lower traps, rhomboids, and core. If you only did one calisthenics exercise for life, this would be it. It is also the pattern you cannot properly train without a bar — which is why a $30 doorway bar is the highest-ROI purchase on this site.

  1. Dead hangGate: 60 s accumulated, one unbroken 30 sCue Grip hard and breathe normally. This builds the grip that otherwise caps your pull-ups.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. This is the Tier 1 purchase that unlocks the phase. Don’t have it? A playground bar, a scaffolding pole, a tree branch thicker than your wrist, or the top of a door frame if your fingertips can take it.

    Grip the bar just outside shoulder width with your thumbs wrapped around it. Hang with straight arms and let your shoulders relax up toward your ears, then pull them down away from your ears and hold. Alternate relaxed and active hanging.

    Most common error Dropping off the bar. Step or lower down — dropping is how wrists and shoulders get tweaked.

    First time Your hands will give out long before your arms do, and the skin on your palms will complain for the first two weeks before it toughens. Wrap your thumbs around the bar. Never drop off — step or lower down.

    Too hard? Keep your toes on the ground and take some weight off. Accumulate the time in short sets.

    Too easy? 60 seconds of accumulated hang, including one unbroken 30 seconds. Then scapular pull-ups.

  2. Scapular pull-upGate: 3 × 10 with a visible 2-inch riseCue Elbows stay locked. If they bend, it has become a tiny pull-up and you have skipped the skill.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. Don’t have it? Same substitutes as the dead hang.

    Hang with completely straight arms. Without bending your elbows at all, pull your shoulder blades down and back so your whole body rises an inch or two. Hold for a second, lower under control.

    Most common error Rushing. This is the position every pull-up starts from, and most failed pull-ups skip it.

    First time An inch or two of movement is the whole rep, and it looks like nothing. This is the position every pull-up starts from, and most failed pull-ups fail because this was skipped.

    Too hard? If your elbows bend it has become a tiny pull-up. Keep your toes down and practise the movement with less weight.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 10 with a visible two-inch rise and locked elbows. Then add band assistance.

  3. Band- or foot-assisted pull-upGate: 3 × 8 on your lightest bandCue Take only as much help as you need — the band should make the last two reps possible, not the whole set easy.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar and a resistance band, or a bar low enough to keep your toes down. Don’t have it? A low bar, a sturdy table edge, or a partner holding your feet. Two chairs and a broomstick work if the broomstick is sound.

    Loop a resistance band over the bar and put one knee or foot in it, or use a bar low enough to keep your toes on the ground. Set your shoulder blades down and back, then pull your elbows toward your ribs until your chin clears the bar. Lower over three seconds.

    Most common error Living on a heavy band forever. Step down a band as soon as you can get eight reps.

    First time Bands snap back. Set your foot in the loop deliberately and keep your face out of the line of the band if it slips.

    Too hard? Use a thicker band. Take only as much help as you need — the band should make the last two reps possible, not the whole set easy.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 on your lightest band. Then negatives.

  4. Negative pull-up (5s down)Gate: 3 × 5, last rep still 5 secondsCue Fight the whole way down, especially the last third where most people just drop.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar and something to step up on. Don’t have it? A chair beside the bar, or jump to the top position.

    Jump or step up so your chin is over the bar. Hold there for a second, then lower yourself for a slow five-count until your arms are completely straight. Step back up and repeat.

    Most common error Only doing negatives. They build the top; you also need assisted reps to learn to start from a dead hang.

    First time Expect real soreness in your lats and biceps for a couple of days the first two times. Also expect your first negative to be much faster than five seconds — that gap is the strength you are building.

    Too hard? If the last rep takes under three seconds, stop the set. Two good negatives beat five falls.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5 where the last rep still takes five seconds. That is your first chin-up.

  5. Chin-up (underhand)Gate: 3 × 5Cue Drive your elbows down into your back pockets rather than pulling your chin up.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. Don’t have it? Same substitutes as the dead hang.

    Palms facing you, hands about shoulder-width. Dead hang, set the shoulder blades, then pull until your chin clears the bar and your chest is close to it. Lower all the way to straight arms.

    Most common error Kipping. Cross your feet, squeeze your glutes, and stay slightly hollow so you cannot swing.

    First time Underhand is easier than overhand because your biceps contribute more. This is deliberate — chin-ups build the pull-up. Getting one is a genuine milestone; most beginners cannot do a single one.

    Too hard? Go back to band assistance for the last set rather than cutting the range short. Chin over the bar or it is not a rep.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5 strict, chin clearly over the bar. Then switch to an overhand grip.

  6. Pull-upGate: 3 × 5Cue Full extension at the bottom of every rep. That is where the strength is built and where people cheat.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. Don’t have it? Same substitutes as the dead hang.

    Palms facing away, hands just outside shoulder width. Dead hang, set the scapula, pull your elbows down and back until your chin is over the bar, then lower over two to three seconds to a full hang.

    Most common error A very wide grip. It shortens the range, stresses the shoulder, and builds nothing extra.

    First time Overhand is harder than underhand and your rep count will fall when you switch. That is the grip change, not lost strength.

    Too hard? Drop back to chin-ups, or band-assist the last set. Kipping is not an easier version, it is a different exercise.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5 strict. Then chase double digits.

  7. Pull-up for repsGate: 3 × 10 strictCue Keep every rep identical. The moment they start getting shorter, the set is finished.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. Don’t have it? Same substitutes as the dead hang.

    The same strict pull-up, now for volume. Rest a full three minutes between sets — if set three is much worse than set one, you rushed the rest.

    Most common error Chasing a rep count by shortening the range as you fatigue.

    First time Ten strict pull-ups puts you past almost everyone who has ever bought a gym membership. From here, added load progresses faster than added reps.

    Too hard? Cut the reps, not the range. Three strict beats eight with a wriggle.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 10 strict. Then add weight or start archer pull-ups.

  8. Weighted / archer pull-upGate: 3 × 6 with 25 lbCue Small jumps. Your lats can handle a big increase; your elbows cannot.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar, plus a backpack with books or a dip belt. Don’t have it? A backpack loaded with hardcovers — roughly 1-2 lb each — or a gallon of water at 8.3 lb.

    Wear a backpack loaded with books or plates, or use a dip belt. Alternatively pull toward one hand while the other arm straightens along the bar. Add 5 lb at a time.

    Most common error Adding weight before you have ten clean bodyweight reps.

    First time Load the backpack tight to your body so it does not swing. A swinging weight turns a strict pull-up into a shoulder-testing arc.

    Too hard? Take weight off before you take range off.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 6 with 25 lb. Beyond this you are into one-arm territory.

Pull-up — grip, setup, execution, and why yours is stalling core lift

Grip choices

  • Pull-up (overhand/pronated), hands just outside shoulder width — most lat, hardest.
  • Chin-up (underhand/supinated), shoulder width — more biceps, noticeably easier. Start here if you can't do a pull-up yet. Most people get a chin-up 2–4 weeks before a pull-up.
  • Neutral (palms facing each other), if your bar has handles — usually the most shoulder- and elbow-friendly of the three.
  • Very wide grip — popular, shortens the range, increases shoulder stress, doesn't build a wider back any faster. Skip it.

Execution

  1. Hang with straight arms. Grip hard — wrap your thumb around the bar.
  2. Set the shoulders first: pull your shoulder blades down and back, away from your ears. This one step fixes more failed pull-ups than anything else.
  3. Squeeze glutes, brace abs, cross or point your feet. Body slightly hollow — do not let your legs swing behind you.
  4. Pull your elbows down toward your ribs, not your chin up toward the bar. Drive them into your back pockets.
  5. Chin clears the bar. Chest toward the bar.
  6. Lower over 2–3 seconds all the way back to straight arms. Full extension every rep.

Why yours is stalling

  • You start each rep dead-limp. Set the scapula first. Practice scapular pull-ups.
  • Grip fails before the back does. Add dead hangs at the end of every session.
  • Only doing negatives. Negatives build the top half; you also need assisted concentric work to learn to pull from a dead hang.
  • Not doing rows. A weak mid-back caps your pull-up. Every session has rows for a reason.
  • Bodyweight climbing too fast. Pull-ups are the exercise most punished by weight gain. If your pull-up stalls while you're bulking, that's expected — check reps-per-pound, not raw reps.

Horizontal pull — the row ladder

Trains: rhomboids, mid and lower traps, rear deltoids, lats, biceps. This is the anti-hunchback pattern and the most-skipped exercise in beginner calisthenics — partly because it's the hardest to improvise. Do not skip it.

  1. Doorway towel rowGate: 3 × 15 with feet well forwardCue Squeeze your shoulder blades together at the end of each pull and hold for a second.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A door and a towel. Nothing else. Don’t have it? A sturdy table edge, a fence rail, or a post. Anything you can grip and lean back from.

    Loop a towel around a solid door handle with the door open, and stand on the hinge side so it cannot swing shut. Hold both ends, walk your feet forward and lean back with straight arms, then pull your chest toward your hands.

    Most common error Only bending your elbows. Without the shoulder-blade squeeze this is a biceps curl.

    First time Loop the towel around the handle with the door open and stand on the hinge side, so the door cannot swing shut on you. This is the free substitute that keeps your back training honest before the bar goes up.

    Too hard? Walk your feet back so you are more upright. The more upright you stand, the easier it is.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with your feet well forward. Then get under a table.

  2. Table row, knees bentGate: 3 × 12Cue Body straight from knees to head — do not let the hips sag toward the floor.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A solid dining table. Don’t have it? A desk, a low bar, or two chairs with a broomstick across them — test it hard first.

    Lie under a sturdy dining table, grip the edge with both hands about shoulder-width, knees bent and feet flat. Brace your core, then pull your chest to the underside of the table and lower under control.

    Most common error Trusting a table you have not tested. Push up on it hard first and weight the far side if it can tip.

    First time Check the table can hold your weight and cannot tip — weigh the far side down if you need to. This is the single most valuable free exercise you have; your back has no other good bodyweight option before the bar.

    Too hard? Walk your feet in toward you and stay more upright. Bent knees with feet close is the easiest version.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with your chest touching the underside. Then straighten your legs.

  3. Inverted row, legs straightGate: 3 × 12Cue Lead with the elbows and finish by pinching the shoulder blades together.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A table, a low bar, or rings at hip height. Don’t have it? A table works for all of this. Rings or a bar are kinder on the wrists.

    Same setup but with your legs straight and only your heels on the floor. Chest touches the bar or table edge every rep.

    Most common error A head-first reach. The chest travels, not the chin.

    First time Your hips will want to sag. One line from heels to head, and your chest touches — not your stomach.

    Too hard? Bend your knees again, or raise the bar. Higher bar equals easier row.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with your chest touching and your body in one line. Then elevate your feet.

  4. Feet-elevated inverted rowGate: 3 × 12Cue Keep the glutes squeezed — the higher the feet, the more the hips want to sag.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A low bar or table, plus a chair for your feet. Don’t have it? Any box, step, or stack of books at roughly bar height.

    Heels on a chair so your body is horizontal or slightly head-down. Everything else is the same, with more of your weight on your arms.

    Most common error Raising the feet before you can do twelve clean flat-footed reps.

    First time With your feet level with the bar this is close to a horizontal bodyweight row, which is a genuinely hard pull. Rep counts drop.

    Too hard? Lower the feet. Every few inches counts.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with feet level with the bar. Then archer rows.

  5. Archer rowGate: 3 × 8 /sideCue Hips stay square. Rotating to reach the bar defeats the purpose.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A low bar or rings. Don’t have it? A table edge, though the hand position is more awkward.

    Take a wide grip. Pull your chest toward one hand while the other arm straightens along the bar, then lower and alternate sides.

    Most common error Letting the straight arm do half the pull.

    First time The straight arm assists more than it feels like it does. Keep the hips square and resist the twist.

    Too hard? Raise the bar, or go back to feet-elevated rows for another month.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 per side with square hips. Then tuck front lever rows.

  6. Tuck front lever rowGate: 3 × 6Cue Hold the tuck. The moment your hips drop it becomes an awkward pull-up.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar or rings. Don’t have it? Rings are much kinder here than a fixed bar.

    Hang, pull into a tuck front lever with your back horizontal and knees to your chest, then row your chest to the bar while holding that shape.

    Most common error Attempting this before you can hold a tuck front lever for twenty seconds.

    First time Holding the tuck while rowing is the hard part, not the row. Your midsection will fail before your back does.

    Too hard? Tuck tighter — knees closer to your chest shortens the lever and takes weight off.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 6 holding the tuck. From here you are working the front lever proper.

The row cue that changes everything. Lead with your elbows and finish by squeezing your shoulder blades together hard, as if pinching a pencil between them, then pause for one second. Rowing with straight-line arm-bend only and no scapular retraction trains biceps and skips the back entirely.

Legs — the squat pattern

Trains: quadriceps, glutes, adductors, and — once you go single-leg — a huge amount of balance and hip stability. Bodyweight squats stop being a strength exercise for a healthy 150 lb person within a few weeks. The answer is single-leg work, which is why the ladder moves there fast.

  1. Box squatGate: 3 × 15Cue Touch, do not flop. Controlling the last inch is the whole point.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair, bench, or bed edge. Don’t have it? A stack of books, a low stool, or a step. Higher is easier.

    Stand in front of a chair, feet shoulder-width and toes turned slightly out. Push your hips back and sit down until you just touch the seat, then stand up by driving through your whole foot.

    Most common error Letting the knees fall inward as you stand. Push them out over your second toe.

    First time Push your hips back first, not down. Touch the seat, do not sit and rest on it. If your heels lift, put a folded towel under them and work on your ankles separately.

    Too hard? Raise the surface. Sitting to a higher box is a legitimate month of training, not a failure.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 touching the seat under control. Then squat without the chair.

  2. Bodyweight squatGate: 3 × 20Cue Heels stay down. If they lift, put a book under them and work on ankle mobility.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Nothing.

    Feet shoulder-width, toes slightly out, arms forward for balance. Sit down between your hips until the crease of your hip is below your kneecap, keeping your heels flat and chest up, then stand.

    Most common error Stopping at parallel because it is easier. Below parallel or it is a partial.

    First time Knees tracking over your toes is fine and normal. Knees collapsing inward is not — push them out as you stand. Most people find depth is limited by their ankles, not their knees.

    Too hard? Put the chair back. There is no shame in a target.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 with your thighs at least parallel to the floor. Then move to one leg at a time.

  3. Split squatGate: 3 × 12 /legCue Straight down, not forward. Your torso stays over your hips.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Nothing. A wall or chair back for balance if you need it.

    Step one foot forward into a stance about two to three feet long, both feet flat and pointing forward. Lower straight down until your back knee is an inch off the floor, then press through the front foot to stand. All reps on one leg, then switch.

    Most common error A stance too short, which jams the front knee and cramps the back leg.

    First time Single-leg work exposes a strength difference between your sides that you probably did not know about. Do your weaker leg first and match the stronger one to it.

    Too hard? Hold a wall with one hand, or shorten the range so your back knee stops higher.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 per leg with your back knee lightly touching down. Then elevate the back foot.

  4. Bulgarian split squatGate: 3 × 12 /legCue Most of your weight on the front foot — the back leg is a kickstand, not a driver.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A chair, bench, or step for your back foot. Don’t have it? A couch, a bed, or a low windowsill. Around knee height.

    Stand two to three feet in front of a chair and place the top of your rear foot on it. Lower straight down until your rear knee nearly touches and your front thigh is at or below parallel, then drive through the whole front foot.

    Most common error Falling over and giving up. Hold a doorframe with one hand for the first few sessions.

    First time This is the hardest leg exercise most people ever do without a barbell, and it is brutal the first time. Expect three or four days of soreness. Start with two sets, not three.

    Too hard? Lower the back foot, or go back to split squats.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 per leg. Then start pistol work.

  5. Assisted / box pistol squatGate: 3 × 8 /leg to a low boxCue Reach both arms forward as a counterweight — it is the difference between standing up and falling backward.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A low box, chair, or stair, and something to hold for balance. Don’t have it? A door frame to hold, or a doorway with a towel through the handles.

    Stand on one leg with the other extended in front. Sit back to a box or chair, tap, and stand back up on that one leg. Lower the box height over weeks, or hold a doorframe with one hand instead.

    Most common error Dropping onto the box. Control the descent or raise the box.

    First time Balance and ankle mobility limit this long before strength does. That is normal and it is why the box and the handhold exist.

    Too hard? Sit to a higher box, or hold on more. Both are real progressions.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 per leg to a low box with minimal hand assistance. Then shrimp squats.

  6. Shrimp squatGate: 3 × 8 /legCue Keep your chest up and the working knee tracking over your toes.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Nothing. A folded towel under the back knee.

    Standing on one leg, bend the other knee and hold that foot behind you with the same-side hand. Lower until the held knee touches the floor, then stand back up.

    Most common error Crashing the back knee into the floor. Put a folded towel down and touch it lightly.

    First time Your back knee touches lightly — it does not crash. Put a folded towel down for the first few weeks while you learn how far away the floor is.

    Too hard? Hold a support, or touch down to a raised pad rather than the floor.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8 per leg with the back knee touching down under control.

  7. Pistol squatGate: 3 × 5 /legCue Arms forward, weight over midfoot. Ankle mobility, not strength, is what stops most people.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Nothing.

    Stand on one leg with the other held straight out in front. Sit all the way down, keeping the free leg off the floor and the heel of the standing foot planted, then stand back up without touching down.

    Most common error Attempting these before you can do eight clean box pistols to a low box.

    First time A full pistol squat is a strength, mobility, and balance feat at once, and it usually takes a beginner a year. If one of the three is missing, train that one specifically rather than grinding the whole movement.

    Too hard? Hold a doorframe, or sit to a low box. Both keep it a pistol squat.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5 per leg, full depth, no assistance.

Bulgarian split squat — the highest-value leg exercise you own core lift

Setup: Stand about 2–3 feet in front of a chair or couch. Place the top of your rear foot on it (laces down) — or the ball of the foot if the top of the foot is uncomfortable. Front foot flat, pointing forward.

Execution: Lower straight down until your rear knee is an inch off the floor and your front thigh is at or below parallel. Keep most of your weight on the front foot — the back leg is a kickstand, not a driver. Press through the whole front foot, especially the heel, back to the top. Do all reps on one leg, then switch.

Adjusting emphasis: a longer stance and a slight forward torso lean hits glutes and hamstrings more; a shorter, more upright stance hits quads more. Do both over time.

Common problems:

  • Falling over. Normal for the first 2–3 sessions. Hold a wall or doorframe with one hand until your stabilizers catch up.
  • Front knee caving inward. Actively push the knee out over your second toe. Add glute bridges to your warm-up.
  • Rear leg cramping. Stance is too short. Step further forward.
  • Too easy at 12 reps? Elevate the front foot on a book for more range, or wear a loaded backpack.

Legs — hinge & hamstrings

Trains: glutes, hamstrings, spinal erectors. Squatting patterns barely touch the hamstrings, and calisthenics athletes are famous for having none. This is the fix, and it also protects your knees and lower back.

  1. Glute bridgeGate: 3 × 20Cue Squeeze the glutes to lift, do not arch the lower back to lift.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor. A mat or carpet is kinder on your spine.

    Lie on your back with knees bent and heels close to your butt, arms flat at your sides. Drive through your heels to lift your hips until your body is a straight line from knees to shoulders, squeeze the glutes hard for two seconds, then lower.

    Most common error Pushing off your toes. Drive through the heels.

    First time You should feel this in your glutes. If you feel it mostly in your lower back or hamstrings, you are arching rather than squeezing — tuck your pelvis before you lift.

    Too hard? Reduce the range — lift to where you can still feel your glutes rather than your lower back.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 with a two-second squeeze at the top. Then move to one leg.

  2. Single-leg glute bridgeGate: 3 × 15 /legCue Hips stay square — if one side drops, lower the range and build up.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Same setup with one foot planted and the other knee pulled toward your chest. Drive up through the planted heel, keeping your hips level side to side.

    Most common error Rushing. Two seconds up, two-second squeeze, two seconds down.

    First time Your hips will want to drop on the unsupported side. Keep them level — that is most of the exercise.

    Too hard? Go back to two legs, or keep the non-working foot lightly touching down.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 per leg with level hips. Then elevate your shoulders.

  3. Shoulder-elevated hip thrustGate: 3 × 15Cue Finish with a hard glute squeeze at the top and ribs down.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A couch, bed, or bench to rest your shoulder blades on. Don’t have it? A sturdy chair pushed against a wall, or a step.

    Sit on the floor with your upper back against the edge of a couch, knees bent, feet flat and about shoulder-width. Drive your hips up until your torso is parallel to the floor, chin tucked, then lower.

    Most common error Hyperextending the lower back at the top instead of finishing with the glutes.

    First time The extra range is the point. Make sure whatever you rest on is against a wall — this is a movement that slides furniture.

    Too hard? Go back to floor bridges, or reduce the range.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with a full lockout and a two-second squeeze. Then sliding leg curls.

  4. Sliding leg curlGate: 3 × 10Cue Hips stay high the entire time. The moment they drop, the hamstrings stop working.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Two towels on a hard floor, or furniture sliders. Don’t have it? Paper plates on carpet, or socks on hardwood. Both work.

    Lie on your back with your heels on towels or furniture sliders on a hard floor. Bridge your hips up, then slowly slide your feet away until your legs are almost straight, and curl them back in — all without letting your hips touch down.

    Most common error Extending further than you can curl back from.

    First time Only works on hardwood, tile, or laminate. Your hamstrings will cramp the first couple of times — that is normal for a muscle that has never been trained through this range.

    Too hard? Use both legs, shorten the range, or push through your hands on the way back out.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 10 keeping your hips up throughout. Then single-leg Romanian deadlifts.

  5. Single-leg Romanian deadliftGate: 3 × 12 /leg, slowCue Hips square to the floor — do not let the free-leg hip rotate open.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Nothing. A wall for fingertip balance. Don’t have it? A backpack with books adds load once bodyweight is easy.

    Stand on one leg with a soft knee. Hinge at the hip, letting the free leg extend straight behind you as your torso lowers, until you feel a strong stretch in the standing hamstring. Keep your back flat, then return by driving the hip forward.

    Most common error Rounding the back to reach lower. Stop where the stretch is, not where your fingers reach.

    First time Push your hips back, do not bend forward at the waist. Your back stays flat and your standing knee stays softly bent. Expect to wobble for the first month.

    Too hard? Touch the free foot down between reps, or hold a wall.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 per leg, slow, with a flat back. Then Nordic negatives.

  6. Nordic curl negativeGate: 3 × 5 with a 4-second lowerCue Hips stay extended — the moment you fold at the hip the hamstrings are off the hook.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Something immovable to hook your heels under, and a folded towel or pillow under your knees. Don’t have it? A partner holding your ankles, a loaded couch, or the underside of a bed frame. Test it holds hard before you trust it.

    Kneel on a folded towel with your ankles wedged under a heavy couch or held by a partner. Keeping your body straight from knees to head, lower your torso forward as slowly as you can, catch yourself with your hands, and push back to the start.

    Most common error Doing three sets of ten the first time. Do one set of three. This causes the worst soreness of anything in the program.

    First time This is a genuinely advanced hamstring exercise and it belongs in the back half of the program, not week one. Your hands catch you — set them up before you start. Expect severe soreness after your first session; do exactly one set the first time.

    Too hard? Push off your hands harder on the way down, or shorten the range and stop at 45 degrees.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 5 with a controlled four-second lower. Then the full Nordic curl.

  7. Full Nordic curlGate: 3 × 3Cue Control the whole range; a fast drop is a strain waiting to happen.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Heel anchor and knee padding. Don’t have it? Same as the negative.

    The same movement, lowering and returning under hamstring power with no push from the hands.

    Most common error Attempting it before you can do five clean four-second negatives.

    First time Very few people get here without months of negatives first. There is no shortcut and the hamstring is a muscle that tears when rushed.

    Too hard? Go back to negatives, or push off your hands to get back up.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 3 under full control, both directions.

Nordic curls cause the worst soreness of any exercise in this program. Do one set of three the first time. Not three sets of ten. People who ignore this cannot walk down stairs for four days.

Calves & feet

Small, easily trained, and completely ignorable until you notice your calves haven't changed in a year. They respond to high reps and a full stretch.

  • Standing calf raise — 3 × 25, full range, 2-second pause at the top.
  • Single-leg calf raise on a stair — heel drops below the step for a deep stretch, rise to full tiptoe. 3 × 15–20 per leg. This is the version that actually works at 150 lb.
  • Seated / bent-knee calf raise — targets the soleus underneath. Put a loaded backpack on your knee while seated. 3 × 20.
  • Tibialis raises — back against a wall, feet out in front, pull your toes up toward your shins. 3 × 20. Prevents shin splints and balances the ankle.

Core — anti-extension

Trains: rectus abdominis, transverse abdominis, obliques, hip flexors. The core's real job in calisthenics is resisting movement — keeping your body a rigid lever. That's why holds beat crunches here.

  1. Dead bugGate: 3 × 10 /side, no lower-back gapCue Press the lower back down so hard nobody could slide a hand under it. If it lifts, shorten the reach.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Lie on your back with arms pointing at the ceiling and knees bent at 90° over your hips. Press your lower back flat into the floor. Slowly lower one arm overhead and the opposite leg toward the floor, then return and switch sides.

    Most common error Going fast. Three seconds out, three seconds back.

    First time Press your lower back flat into the floor and keep it there. If it lifts, you have reached too far — that is the entire skill, and it transfers to every other movement on this site.

    Too hard? Reach less far, or keep your knees bent. Range is the dial here.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 10 per side with no gap under your lower back. Then hold a plank.

  2. PlankGate: 3 × 60 sCue Actively brace as if bracing for a punch — a plank you can hold for three minutes is not a plank, it is a rest.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor. A mat helps your elbows.

    Forearms on the floor under your shoulders, feet together, body in one line. Squeeze the glutes, tuck the pelvis slightly, pull the ribs down, and push the floor away so the upper back is not collapsed.

    Most common error Adding time past sixty seconds. Add difficulty instead.

    First time A plank is not about how long — it is about how hard. Squeeze your glutes, tuck your pelvis, and pull your elbows toward your toes. Done properly, 60 seconds is plenty.

    Too hard? Drop to your knees, or put your forearms on a chair. Shorten the hold before you break the line.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 60 seconds in a straight line with the pelvis tucked. Then tuck hollow holds.

  3. Tuck hollow holdGate: 3 × 45 sCue Lower back glued to the floor. That contact is the entire exercise.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Lie on your back and press your lower back flat. Lift your shoulders and head slightly off the floor, arms reaching forward, and hold your knees tucked toward your chest.

    Most common error Straining the neck. Keep a tennis-ball-sized gap under your chin.

    First time Lower back pressed flat is the requirement. The moment a gap appears, the set is over — no matter how long you have held it.

    Too hard? Tuck tighter, or put your hands on your thighs.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 45 seconds with your lower back glued to the floor. Then extend one leg.

  4. One-leg hollow holdGate: 3 × 45 sCue Extend only as far as you can keep the back flat.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    From the tuck hollow, extend one leg out straight and low while keeping the other tucked. Alternate legs between sets.

    Most common error Extending both legs before one is easy.

    First time Swap sides between sets. The lower the extended leg, the harder it gets — that is your fine adjustment.

    Too hard? Bend the extended leg, or raise it higher. Higher is easier.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 45 seconds per side with a flat lower back. Then extend both legs.

  5. Full hollow holdGate: 3 × 45 sCue The lower the arms and legs, the harder it gets — use that as the dial.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Arms extended overhead by your ears, legs straight and low, shoulders and heels both off the floor, lower back pressed flat. The most transferable core position in calisthenics.

    Most common error Letting the back arch to get the legs lower. Raise the legs instead.

    First time This is the shape underneath the handstand, the front lever, and a good push-up. Time spent here pays out across the whole site.

    Too hard? Raise your legs and arms higher, or tuck one knee.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 45 seconds. Then start rocking.

  6. Hollow rockGate: 3 × 20Cue The body stays one rigid banana-shaped piece; only the contact point moves.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor. A mat or carpet — this is uncomfortable on bare boards.

    Hold a rigid full hollow shape and rock back and forth on your lower back, driven from the shoulders, without changing the shape at all.

    Most common error Piking to generate the rock.

    First time The shape never changes; only the point of contact moves. If you find yourself piking at the hips to generate the rock, you are not ready.

    Too hard? Go back to the static hold. Rocking a shape you cannot hold just teaches a worse shape.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 with the shape unchanged throughout. Then long-lever planks or an ab wheel.

  7. Long-lever plank / ab wheelGate: 3 × 30 s or 3 × 10Cue Ribs down and glutes squeezed the whole way. Range comes later.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor, or an ab wheel (about $20). Don’t have it? A barbell with plates rolls the same way. So does a towel on a hard floor.

    Walk your elbows well out in front of your shoulders and hold. Or kneel with an ab wheel and roll out only as far as you can return from with a flat back.

    Most common error Rolling out until the lower back arches. That is where people hurt themselves.

    First time The ab wheel is one of the few genuinely worthwhile cheap purchases. Start with a very short range — this is the exercise people most reliably hurt their lower back on by going too far on day one.

    Too hard? Shorten the reach, or roll out to a wall stop and gradually move the wall further away.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 30 seconds, or 3 sets of 10 full roll-outs.

How to find the hollow position. Lie on your back and press your lower back into the floor so hard that someone couldn't slide a hand underneath. That is the position. Now lift your shoulders and legs while keeping it. The moment your lower back lifts off, you've gone too far — bend your knees more or lower your legs less. Everyone cheats this. Don't.

Core — leg raises & the L-sit

  1. Lying knee tuckGate: 3 × 20Cue Keep the lower back pressed down throughout.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Lie on your back with your hands tucked under your hips for support. Pull your knees toward your chest, lifting your hips slightly off the floor at the top, then lower under control without letting your feet touch down.

    Most common error Swinging the legs to build momentum.

    First time Lower back stays flat. Hands under your tailbone is a legitimate assist, not cheating.

    Too hard? Put your hands under your tailbone, or reduce the range.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 with your lower back flat. Then straighten your legs.

  2. Lying straight-leg raiseGate: 3 × 15Cue Stop lowering at the point where the back would arch — that point is your current range.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor.

    Same position with legs straight. Raise them to vertical, then lower until just before your lower back lifts off the floor, and raise again.

    Most common error Letting the back arch to get the legs lower. That is where lower-back pain comes from.

    First time The moment your lower back arches off the floor, the set is over. That point is usually much higher than people expect.

    Too hard? Bend your knees slightly, or stop the legs higher off the floor.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with your heels reaching the floor and your back still flat. Then take it to the bar.

  3. Hanging knee raiseGate: 3 × 15Cue Pause for a full second at the bottom of every rep to kill the swing.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. Don’t have it? A playground bar, or dip bars in a support hold.

    Hang from the bar with shoulders active. Raise your knees above hip height, curling the pelvis slightly at the top, then lower slowly to a full hang.

    Most common error Using a body swing to throw the knees up.

    First time Your grip will fail before your abs do for the first month. That is normal and it is why dead hangs are in the program. Control the swing — if you are pendulum-ing, you are using momentum.

    Too hard? Tuck tighter and raise less high. Or go back to the floor version until your grip catches up.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 with no swing. Then straighten your legs.

  4. Hanging straight-leg raiseGate: 3 × 12Cue Point your toes and keep the legs locked — bent knees are the easier version.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar. Don’t have it? Same as above.

    Hang with active shoulders and legs straight, toes pointed. Keeping the knees locked, raise your legs to horizontal or above, curling the pelvis slightly at the top, then lower over three seconds to a full hang and pause before the next rep.

    Most common error Kipping. If you cannot do it strictly, go back to knee raises.

    First time Hamstring flexibility limits this as much as strength does. If you cannot reach horizontal with straight legs, that is usually your hamstrings talking.

    Too hard? Bend the knees. Any amount of bend takes load off.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 12 with straight legs to horizontal. Then toes to bar.

  5. Toes to barGate: 3 × 8Cue Lean back slightly and pull with the lats to get the last part of the range.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A pull-up bar with room above and in front. Don’t have it? Same as above.

    From a hang, raise your legs all the way until your feet touch the bar between your hands, then lower with control.

    Most common error Turning it into a kipping swing to reach the bar.

    First time Strict toes to bar is genuinely hard. The version you see done fast with a kip is a different exercise; do not compare yourself to it.

    Too hard? Stop at horizontal. Toes to bar is a long way past horizontal.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 8, toes touching the bar, without swinging.

The L-sit ladder

  1. Foot-supported L-sitGate: 3 × 20 sCue Push your shoulders down away from your ears. That is the strength you are actually building.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor, or two books to raise your hands. Don’t have it? Parallettes, push-up handles, or the edges of two chairs make this far easier on the wrists.

    Sit on the floor with legs straight, hands flat beside your hips (or on two books). Push down hard through your hands to lift your hips off the floor, keeping your heels lightly touching.

    Most common error Shrugging up into your ears, which is what happens the moment you tire.

    First time Raising your hands is the main difficulty dial for the entire L-sit ladder. Books, parallettes, or chair edges all buy you months of progression.

    Too hard? Raise your hands higher, or keep more weight on your feet.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 seconds with your hands taking most of the weight. Then tuck your knees up.

  2. Tuck L-sitGate: 3 × 20 sCue Straight, locked arms and a tall push through the shoulders.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallettes, push-up handles, two books, or the floor. Don’t have it? Two stacks of hardcovers, or the edges of two sturdy chairs.

    Same setup, but tuck your knees to your chest and lift your feet completely clear of the floor. Hold.

    Most common error Letting the hips sag back below the hands.

    First time Push the floor away and press your shoulders down away from your ears. Most people fail their first tuck L-sit at the shoulders, not the abs.

    Too hard? Raise your hands, or lift one foot at a time.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 20 seconds with both feet clear of the floor. Then extend one leg.

  3. One-leg-extended L-sitGate: 3 × 15 s /sideCue Extend the leg without letting the hips drop.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallettes, handles, or books. Don’t have it? Chair edges.

    From the tuck, extend one leg straight out horizontally while keeping the other tucked. Alternate between sets.

    Most common error Rushing to both legs before one is solid.

    First time Alternate sides between sets. Hamstring tightness is often the real limit here rather than strength.

    Too hard? Tuck the extended leg. Any bend takes load off.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 seconds per side. Then extend both legs.

  4. Full L-sitGate: 3 × 15 sCue Tight hamstrings, not weak abs, are what usually stop the legs straightening here.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Parallettes, handles, or the floor. Don’t have it? Chair edges, or the floor once you are strong enough to clear it.

    Both legs straight out horizontally, toes pointed, arms locked, shoulders depressed, chest up.

    Most common error Bending the knees slightly and calling it a full L-sit. Film it from the side.

    First time A clean 15-second L-sit is a real milestone and it takes most people six months or more. Legs level, knees locked, shoulders down.

    Too hard? Bend one or both knees. Raise your hands.

    Too easy? 3 sets of 15 seconds with legs level and knees locked.

Why the L-sit is worth the trouble: it simultaneously trains the abs, hip flexors, quads, triceps and — crucially — scapular depression, the strength of pushing your shoulders down away from your ears. That is the exact quality that makes handstands, dips, and levers possible. It also needs almost no space, so it's the perfect skill to practice at home. Note that tight hamstrings will limit your L-sit before your core does; if your legs won't straighten, that's a flexibility problem, not a strength one.

Core — anti-rotation & lower back

  • Side plank — 3 × 45 s per side. Progress by lifting the top leg or extending the top arm overhead.
  • Pallof press (needs a band) — anchor a band at chest height to your side, hold it at your sternum, press straight out and resist the rotation. 3 × 12 per side. Best anti-rotation exercise there is.
  • Bird dog — on all fours, extend opposite arm and leg, hold 3 s, keep hips square. 3 × 10 per side.
  • Superman hold — face down, lift chest and legs, squeeze glutes. 3 × 30 s. Balances all the front-side work.
  • Reverse hyperextension on a couch — hips at the edge, torso supported, lift legs to horizontal. 3 × 15.
  • Suitcase carry — walk 40 yards holding a heavy bag in one hand, staying perfectly upright. Underrated, and you can do it with groceries.

Skills — handstand, front lever, muscle-up

Skills are practiced fresh, before your strength work, well short of failure, and often — 3×/week minimum. They are motor learning, not fatigue accumulation. Ten focused minutes beats one exhausting hour.

Handstand

  1. Plank → pike holdGate: 60 s plank, 45 s pikeCue Push tall through the shoulders in both positions.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor and a wall.

    Hold a straight-arm plank, then walk your feet in and hold a high pike with your hips stacked over your shoulders. Alternate the two.

    Most common error Skipping the wrist prep beforehand. Every session, two minutes.

    First time This is the entry to the handstand line, and it is deliberately unglamorous. The shape you build here is the shape you will hold upside down.

    Too hard? Shorten the hold, or bend your knees in the pike.

    Too easy? A 60-second plank and a 45-second pike hold. Then start wall walks.

  2. Wall walkGate: walk in and back out 5 timesCue Small steps with the hands, and never go further in than you can come back from.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall and four feet of floor.

    Start in a push-up position with feet against the wall. Walk your feet up and your hands in until your chest is close to the wall, then walk back out under control.

    Most common error Sagging through the middle. Ribs down, glutes on.

    First time Move anything breakable first. Being inverted spikes your heart rate and feels alarming for the first two or three sessions, then stops.

    Too hard? Walk in less far. Only go as far in as you can walk back out from.

    Too easy? Walk all the way in and back out five times. Then hold at the wall.

  3. Chest-to-wall handstand holdGate: 60 sCue A straight line from hands through shoulders, hips and heels. This is the shape you are training.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall.

    Walk up into a vertical handstand facing the wall, hands a hand-span away from it. Squeeze the glutes, tuck the pelvis, pull the ribs down, push tall, and look at the floor between your hands.

    Most common error Practising back-to-wall instead. It teaches an arched banana you then have to unlearn.

    First time Chest to wall, never back to wall. Back-to-wall teaches an arched banana shape that takes months to unlearn once it is a habit.

    Too hard? Hands further from the wall.

    Too easy? 60 seconds of accumulated hold. Then practise kicking up.

  4. Kick-up to wallGate: 10 controlled kick-upsCue Kick just hard enough to reach vertical. Over-kicking is a habit that is hard to break later.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall.

    Back to the wall, hands about a foot from it. Step one foot forward, kick the other up and follow with the first, landing your heels lightly on the wall. Practise controlling the kick so you never slam into it.

    Most common error Never practising the bail. Learn to turn sideways and step down before you go freestanding.

    First time Learn the bail before you need it: turn sideways and step down. Practise it deliberately, because you will need it the first time you over-kick.

    Too hard? Kick up more gently and accept not reaching vertical. A controlled short kick beats a wild one.

    Too easy? 10 controlled kick-ups where you never slam into the wall. Then start taking your heels off.

  5. Heel pulls / toe pullsGate: 3-second free holdCue Balance comes from your fingers — press through the fingertips when falling forward, through the heel of the hand when falling back.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment A clear wall.

    From the chest-to-wall hold, peel one foot off the wall, then the other, and hold your balance for a moment before touching back.

    Most common error Trying to correct with your hips. Correct with your hands.

    First time Balance comes from your fingers, not your hips. Press through the fingertips when you fall forward, through the heel of the hand when you fall back.

    Too hard? Take one heel off at a time rather than both.

    Too easy? A three-second free hold. Then go freestanding.

  6. Freestanding handstandGate: 30 sCue Small constant corrections, not big saves.
    Setup & execution

    Equipment Floor, ideally something forgiving. A clear space. Don’t have it? Grass or a mat outdoors is more forgiving than a hardwood floor.

    Kick up away from the wall into the same straight shape, and correct continuously with fingertip pressure.

    Most common error Practising when tired. A tired handstand teaches the wrong movement.

    First time Small constant corrections, not big saves. Never practise this tired — a tired handstand teaches the wrong movement and the practice actively sets you back.

    Too hard? Go back to the wall. The wall is a tool, not a crutch.

    Too easy? A 30-second freestanding hold.

Learn to bail on day one. If you're falling forward, turn your body sideways and step down — a cartwheel exit. Practice it deliberately ten times before you ever try a free handstand. The fear of falling is what stops most people from ever getting off the wall, and knowing you have an exit removes it.

Front lever

Prerequisites before you start: 8 strict pull-ups, 30-second active hang, solid full hollow hold. Straight-arm work loads the elbows and biceps tendon extremely hard — this is the classic overuse injury in calisthenics, and it comes from starting the lever too early.

  1. Active/arched hangHang with shoulder blades pulled down and back, chest up. Gate: 3 × 30 s.
  2. Tuck front leverHanging, pull your body until your back is horizontal with knees tucked to chest, arms straight. Gate: 3 × 20 s.
  3. Advanced tuckOpen the knees to ~90° so your thighs come away from your chest. Gate: 3 × 15 s.
  4. Single-leg front leverOne leg extended, one tucked. Gate: 3 × 10 s per side.
  5. Straddle front leverBoth legs straight and wide. Gate: 3 × 10 s.
  6. Full front leverBody fully straight and horizontal. A genuine multi-year goal.

Muscle-up

Prerequisites, all of them, before you attempt one:

  • 10 strict pull-ups.
  • 10 strict parallel-bar dips.
  • 5 explosive pull-ups where you pull the bar to your lower chest / sternum, not your chin.
  • A false grip you can hold (thumb over the bar, wrist on top) — or the willingness to learn the transition without it.

Then drill the transition specifically: jumping muscle-ups from a low bar, negative muscle-ups (start in the support position, lower slowly through the transition), and straight-bar transitions with band assistance. The muscle-up is 20% strength and 80% learning to rotate your elbows and shoulders around the bar at the top of the pull. Attempting it before the prerequisites just produces flailing and shoulder pain.

Filming your own form — the self-check lists

You cannot see your own form, and the way something feels from the inside is a poor guide to what it looks like. Sagging hips feel level. Half-range pull-ups feel full. This is the closest thing to having a coach that costs nothing: film one working set from the side every two to four weeks, then run it against the checklist for that lift.

How to film so the video is actually useful

  • Side view, at hip height. Prop the phone on a stack of books about 6–8 feet away. Front-on hides almost every error worth finding; the side view shows spine position, range, and joint angles.
  • Whole body in frame, including hands and feet, for the entire set.
  • Film a real working set, not a demo rep. Errors appear under fatigue — reps 6–10 are the informative ones.
  • Use slow motion if you're checking tempo or a specific sticking point.
  • Keep the clips. A push-up from week 1 next to week 12 is more convincing than any measurement.
  • Watch it twice: once for range of motion, once for everything else. Trying to see everything at once means you see nothing.

Universal checks — run these on any lift

  • Did every rep reach the same end position? Compare rep 1 to the last rep. Range shrinking under fatigue is the most common and most self-deceiving error.
  • Is the lowering controlled or falling? Count the seconds. If the eccentric is under one second, you're throwing away half of every rep.
  • Is the spine holding one shape throughout? Any point where the lower back suddenly arches or the upper back rounds is where the core gave out.
  • Are the two sides doing the same thing? One shoulder rising, one hip shifting, one knee caving. Asymmetry under load is worth addressing early.
  • Is there a pause and a lurch? A hitch at one point in the range is a weak position — that's where pauses and partials should be applied.
