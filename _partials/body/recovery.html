Recovery & injury

Training is the stimulus. Recovery is where you actually grow.

Nothing improves during a workout — you leave a session temporarily weaker than you arrived. The adaptation happens in the 24 to 72 hours afterward, and only if you sleep, eat, and give the tissue a chance. This page is about not sabotaging the part that does the work.

Sleep — the protocol

Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool available and it is free. Restricting sleep to 5–6 hours a night measurably reduces muscle protein synthesis, lowers testosterone, raises cortisol, increases the proportion of weight gained as fat rather than muscle, degrades reaction time and coordination (which matters for handstands), and roughly doubles injury risk in athletes. No supplement, program, or diet compensates for it.

Target
7–9 hrs
Actual sleep, not time in bed. Budget ~30 min extra for falling asleep.
Consistency
±30 min
Same bedtime and wake time daily, weekends included. Regularity matters nearly as much as duration.
Bedroom
65–68 °F
Cool, dark, quiet. Core temperature has to drop for deep sleep to happen.

What actually helps, in order of effect

  1. A fixed wake time. Anchor this first, even after a bad night. Your bedtime will follow within a week or two.
  2. No caffeine after ~2 pm. Caffeine has a half-life of 5–6 hours, so a 4 pm coffee still has a quarter of its dose active at midnight. You may fall asleep fine and still lose deep sleep.
  3. Dark and cool. Blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Even small amounts of light suppress melatonin.
  4. Dim lights and screens down 30–60 min before bed. Not because blue light is uniquely evil, but because bright light and stimulating content delay sleep onset.
  5. No large meals or alcohol within 2–3 hours. Alcohol is the worst offender — it puts you to sleep fast and then destroys the second half of the night.
  6. Get morning daylight within an hour of waking, 10 minutes outside. This is the strongest signal for setting your circadian clock, which is what determines when you can fall asleep at night.
  7. Don't train hard within 2 hours of bed if it leaves you wired. Some people are fine; if you're not, move the session earlier.
  8. Naps: 20 minutes, or 90 minutes, before 3 pm. The in-between length wakes you in deep sleep and leaves you groggy.
If you sleep 6 hours a night, fix that before changing anything else about your training. Adding sets, buying creatine, or optimizing your macros while chronically under-slept is rearranging furniture in a burning house. This is the highest-leverage change available to most beginners and it costs nothing but scheduling.

Rest days & active recovery

You train Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. The other four days are not wasted — they are when the adaptation happens. What you do on them still matters.

Soreness (DOMS) vs. injury

Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness peaks 24–48 hours after training, especially after new exercises or lots of eccentric work. It is a normal response to unfamiliar loading. It is not a measure of how good the workout was — experienced trainees making excellent progress are rarely very sore.

What actually reduces DOMS: gradual progression (the real answer), light movement, sleep, and adequate protein. What doesn't: static stretching before or after, and — despite how good it feels — most of what's sold as recovery gear. Ice baths reduce soreness but also blunt some of the adaptation signal, so they are the wrong tool while you're trying to build muscle.

Severe soreness plus dark urine is a medical emergency. Extreme, disproportionate soreness (usually after a wildly excessive first session), swelling, weakness, and cola-colored urine can indicate rhabdomyolysis. Go to an emergency room. It is rare, but it happens specifically to enthusiastic beginners who do 300 push-ups on day one.

The pain traffic light

Green — keep training

  • Muscle burn during a set
  • General muscle ache for 1–3 days after
  • Stiffness that warms out in 5 minutes
  • Fatigue that goes away after a rest day

Yellow — modify now

  • A specific joint aches during one movement but not others
  • Mild discomfort that's still there the next morning
  • Pain at the very end of a range of motion
  • Something that's been "a little off" for more than a week

Do: drop back a rung, shorten the range, reduce sets, extend the warm-up, and swap in a pain-free variation of the same pattern. Do not push through and hope.

Red — stop

  • Sharp, stabbing, or electric pain
  • Pain right on a joint line or a tendon
  • Numbness or tingling down an arm or leg
  • Anything that popped, and then hurt
  • Visible swelling or bruising
  • Pain that gets worse as you warm up
  • Loss of strength or range of motion you can't explain

Do: stop that movement entirely. Train everything else that's pain-free. If it isn't clearly improving within 7–10 days, see a physical therapist or a sports-medicine doctor. Early is cheap; late is not.

The most useful principle in the whole injury section. An injury almost never means you should stop training — it means you should stop training that one movement. Sore elbow? Train legs and core. Tweaked knee? Train upper body. Continuing to train around an injury preserves the habit, keeps the rest of your body progressing, and generally improves the injured area's recovery through better circulation and less deconditioning.

Tendons, and why they lag behind muscle

Muscle adapts to training in weeks. Tendons, ligaments, and joint capsules adapt in months — they have far less blood supply and a much slower collagen turnover. That gap is why the classic calisthenics injury happens at month 4–8, not month 1: your muscles got strong enough to attempt something your connective tissue was never prepared for.

What protects tendons:

  • The rep gates in the ladders. They are deliberately conservative. Clearing 3×12 before moving on isn't about your muscles — it's about accumulating enough time under load for the tendon to remodel.
  • Slow eccentrics. Controlled lowering is one of the best-evidenced tendon-strengthening stimuli there is.
  • Isometric holds. Long holds (30–45 s) at moderate intensity build tendon stiffness and often reduce tendon pain immediately. Support holds, dead hangs, and L-sits do double duty here.
  • Gradual, boring progression. Adding one rep per week is a tendon-friendly rate. Jumping two rungs because you felt strong is not.
  • Full range of motion, which loads the tendon across its whole length rather than one spot.
  • Not skipping warm-ups. Cold connective tissue is stiffer and injures more easily.

The five injuries you're most likely to get

1 · Wrist pain most common

Why: push-ups, planks, and handstands hold the wrist in deep extension under load — a position it never otherwise sees. Almost every beginner gets some version of this.

Prevent: do the wrist prep sequence every single session. It is two minutes and it prevents most cases.

Work around it: do push-ups on fists (neutral wrist), on parallettes or push-up handles, or on dumbbells. Reduce handstand volume. Spread your fingers and grip the floor rather than letting the weight sit on the heel of the hand.

Rehab: gentle wrist flexion and extension against light resistance, progressive loaded wrist rocks, and forearm massage. Usually resolves in 2–4 weeks with prep plus modification.

2 · Elbow tendinopathy — golfer's or tennis elbow months 3–9

Why: too much straight-arm work too early (levers, planche attempts), a sudden jump in pull-up volume, or hanging with completely passive shoulders. Pain on the inside of the elbow is the medial (golfer's) version — the common one in calisthenics.

Prevent: earn the front lever prerequisites before starting it. Warm up with band pull-aparts and light hangs. Don't double your pull volume in one week.

Work around it: switch to a neutral or underhand grip, reduce range, cut volume by half. Rings, which let the elbow rotate freely, are usually more comfortable than a fixed bar.

Rehab: slow, heavy-ish eccentric wrist curls (3×15, 4-second lowering, daily-ish) plus isometric holds. This has strong evidence behind it. Expect 6–12 weeks, and don't abandon the protocol the week it starts feeling better.

3 · Shoulder impingement / rotator cuff irritation

Why: too much pressing relative to pulling, flared elbows in push-ups, dips going too deep, very wide-grip pull-ups, and generally weak scapular control.

Prevent: match or exceed push volume with pull volume. Keep push-up elbows at 45°. Stop dips at parallel. Include band pull-aparts and face-pulls in every warm-up. Learn scapular retraction and depression.

Work around it: narrow your grip, reduce depth, use a neutral grip, drop overhead work temporarily. Keep rowing — rowing usually helps.

Rehab: external rotations with a light band, prone Y-T-W raises, scapular push-ups and scapular pulls, and serratus work (push-up plus). If there's night pain or you can't raise your arm overhead, get it looked at.

4 · Lower back pain

Why: sagging hips in push-ups and planks, leg raises done with an arched lower back, and weak glutes forcing the erectors to do the hip's job.

Prevent: brace properly — ribs down, pelvis slightly tucked, glutes squeezed — on every plank, push-up, and hollow hold. Never let the lower back arch off the floor during a hollow hold or leg raise; regress instead.

Work around it: swap leg raises for dead bugs, hollow holds for tuck holds, and add glute bridges and bird dogs.

Red flags — see a doctor: pain radiating down a leg, numbness or tingling, weakness in the foot, or any change in bladder or bowel function.

5 · Knee pain (usually patellofemoral)

Why: jumping into pistol squats before the required ankle mobility and single-leg strength exist; knees caving inward; a large sudden jump in leg volume.

Prevent: progress the squat ladder in order. Actively push the knee out over the second toe. Build ankle mobility with ankle rocks. Train hamstrings and glutes, not just quads.

Work around it: reduce depth to a pain-free range, use box squats and split squats instead of deep single-leg work, and add glute bridges and hamstring work.

Rehab: quad strengthening in a pain-free range, glute medius work (side-lying leg raises, banded walks), and ankle mobility. Knee pain that's worse going down stairs is the classic patellofemoral pattern.

Training around an injury

An injury almost never means stop training. It means stop training one pattern while everything else continues. Continuing to train the rest of your body preserves the habit, keeps the other patterns progressing, and generally speeds recovery of the injured area through better circulation and less deconditioning.

The pain-monitoring rule. Rehab research generally allows working into mild pain rather than demanding zero. A practical version: on a 0–10 scale, pain during the exercise up to about 3 is acceptable; it must settle back to baseline within 24 hours, and must not be worse the next morning. If it climbs above 3, lingers past a day, or is worse the following morning, you did too much — cut the range, the load, or the volume and rebuild. Sharp, joint-line, or radiating pain is red regardless of the number.

Coming back after time off

Two things are true and people usually only believe the second one. First: you lose far less than you fear. Meaningful muscle loss takes considerably longer than a couple of weeks off, and what you lose returns much faster than it was built — retained myonuclei mean the tissue is primed to regrow. Second: your tendons and your soreness tolerance detrain faster than your strength does, which is why the first week back injures people who go straight to their old numbers.

So the rule for every return is the same: come back at the volume you left, minus a third to a half, and at the same rungs — not harder ones. Rebuild over two to three weeks. Do not test your maxes in week one. The urge to prove you haven't lost anything is exactly the urge that gets people hurt.

After an illness

  • Wait until you are a full day symptom-free before training at all, then return at half volume for the first session.
  • After a fever specifically, give it two to three days beyond symptom resolution. Training with or immediately after a fever is the one scenario with a genuine cardiac risk.
  • Expect your heart rate to be elevated and everything to feel harder for a week. That's normal and it resolves.
  • Prioritize sleep and calories over training volume while you recover. Your immune system is spending the budget.

After an injury

Returning from a specific injury is different from returning from a break, because the tissue has to be reloaded deliberately rather than just re-exposed:

  1. Find your pain-free entry point. Regress until you can do a version of the movement with pain at or below 3/10 that settles by the next morning. That might be four rungs down. It doesn't matter.
  2. Start with isometrics. Long holds (30–45 seconds) at moderate effort are well tolerated by irritated tendons and often reduce pain immediately.
  3. Add slow eccentrics next — controlled 3–4 second lowering. This is the best-evidenced tendon-loading stimulus there is.
  4. Then full range, then volume, then difficulty — in that order, one variable at a time, roughly one change per week.
  5. Keep training everything else hard the entire time. The rest of your body has no reason to detrain.
  6. Expect it to take longer than you want. Tendinopathy commonly needs 6–12 weeks of consistent loading, and the point where it stops hurting is the middle of the process, not the end. Most reinjuries happen when people stop the protocol the week it starts feeling better.
When to stop self-managing. See a physical therapist or sports-medicine doctor if: it isn't clearly improving after 7–10 days of sensible modification; there's numbness, tingling, or weakness; you can't put the joint through its normal range; there was a pop followed by swelling; or it keeps coming back every time you return to normal training. One appointment usually replaces months of guessing, and early is much cheaper than late.

Dynamic — leg swings, arm circles, movement through a rangeBefore training, alwaysRaises tissue temperature and prepares the range you're about to use. No downside.

Stretching does not prevent injury or reduce soreness — that's a long-standing myth. Stretch because you want more range of motion (which you do — handstands, deep squats, and L-sits all demand it), not as injury insurance. Progressive, well-managed loading is the actual injury insurance.

Fatigue management & deloads

Fatigue accumulates faster than you perceive it. A deload every 6 weeks is scheduled into the program — half the sets, 4–5 RIR, keep the skill work. Take an unscheduled one whenever two or more of these are true for a week:

  • Reps declining across multiple exercises
  • Joints aching before the warm-up even starts
  • Resting heart rate up 5+ bpm
  • Sleep getting worse despite being tired
  • Motivation dropping for sessions you normally enjoy
  • Getting sick more often than usual

Signs you're doing too much

True clinical overtraining syndrome is rare and takes months of serious abuse to reach. Overreaching — doing more than you're currently recovering from — is common and easy to fix. Watch for:

Physical

  • Performance dropping for 2+ weeks despite consistent effort
  • Soreness that never fully clears
  • Elevated resting heart rate
  • Frequent minor illnesses
  • Nagging aches in multiple joints at once
  • Loss of appetite

Mental

  • Dreading sessions you used to look forward to
  • Irritability, low mood, poor concentration
  • Sleep getting worse the more tired you get
  • Feeling like you need to do more because results stalled

The fix is almost always the same: one deload week, then return at 75% of the previous volume and rebuild over three weeks. Not a full week off, not a program change, and definitely not more volume.

Stress counts as training load

Your body doesn't have separate recovery budgets for exams, a bad week at work, family stress, and squats. It has one. Chronically elevated cortisol from life stress directly impairs recovery, reduces protein synthesis, degrades sleep quality, and increases injury risk — through exactly the same pathways as excessive training.

Practically: during a genuinely stressful stretch, reduce training volume by about a third rather than trying to power through. Keep the three sessions — the habit and the mood benefit are real — but drop a set from each exercise and stay further from failure. You'll maintain almost everything, and you'll come out the other side able to push again. Trying to set records during finals week is how people get hurt and quit.
