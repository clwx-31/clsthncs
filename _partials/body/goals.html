Goals & metrics

How to prove to yourself that it's working

Progress in the first year is real but slow enough to be invisible day to day. The only defense against quitting is measurement — numbers that you can compare against your past self. This page tells you exactly what to record, how often, and what "good" looks like at every stage.

The baseline test — do this on day one

Twenty minutes, once, before you train. These numbers are irreplaceable: in six months you will badly want to know where you started, and there is no way to go back and get them. Repeat the identical test at weeks 9, 12, and 24, and then every 12 weeks forever.

Protocol

  1. Warm up for 5 minutes — light cardio plus wrist and shoulder prep. Don't test cold.
  2. Rest 3 full minutes between every test. This is a strength test, not a circuit.
  3. One attempt per test. Every rep must be full range with clean form — a half rep is not a rep. Stop the set the moment form breaks, not when you physically cannot move.
  4. Test in this exact order every time. Order affects results, so it has to be constant.
  5. If you can't do a movement at all, write 0. Zero is a real, useful data point — it's the one that will look most dramatic later.

Do not measure daily what changes monthly. Weighing daily is fine because you average it. Taking photos or tape measurements weekly is actively harmful — the change is smaller than the measurement noise, so all you see is randomness, and it reads as failure. Four-week intervals, minimum.

Progress photos — the protocol

You look at yourself every day, so you are the person least able to notice your own change. Photos four weeks apart are shockingly effective, but only if the conditions are constant. Otherwise you're comparing lighting, not muscle.

  • Same time of day — first thing in the morning, fasted, after the bathroom. Food and water visibly change your midsection.
  • Same place, same light. Overhead room lighting, not a window — daylight changes with the season and time.
  • Same clothing — shorts or underwear, no shirt. Same clothes every time.
  • Same phone position — mark a spot on the floor with tape, prop the phone at the same height, use a 10-second timer.
  • Four shots: front relaxed, side relaxed, back relaxed, and front flexed. The relaxed ones are the honest ones; the flexed one is the fun one.
  • Stand normally. Don't suck in on day one and pose hard on day ninety, or you'll fool yourself in both directions.
  • Never delete the early ones, even if you hate them. Especially if you hate them.

Estimating body fat without calipers or a scan

The US Navy circumference method needs only a tape and gets within a few percentage points for most people — accurate enough to track a direction, which is all you need. For men: %BF = 86.010 × log₁₀(waist − neck) − 70.041 × log₁₀(height) + 36.76, all in inches.

Why the pull-up targets look modest. Pull-ups are the exercise most punished by bodyweight — you're lifting every pound you gain. Going from 0 to 8 pull-ups while adding 12 lb of bodyweight is a much larger strength gain than the rep count suggests. If you want a number that isn't confounded, track reps × bodyweight: 3 pull-ups at 155 lb (465) versus 8 at 162 lb (1,296) is nearly a tripling of work capacity.

Leading vs. lagging indicators

Beginners obsess over lagging indicators — the mirror, the scale — and get discouraged because those move slowest. Watch the leading indicators instead; they respond within days, and they are what cause the lagging ones.

Leading — check weekly

  • Sessions completed this week (target: 3)
  • Total reps beaten vs. last week
  • Protein hit on how many days (target: 6/7)
  • Calories hit on how many days
  • Nights with 7+ hours of sleep (target: 6/7)
  • Whether you actually logged every set

Lagging — check monthly or quarterly

  • Bodyweight 7-day average trend
  • Tape measurements
  • Progress photos
  • Baseline retest results
  • Milestones ticked
  • How you look in the mirror

If all six leading indicators are green for eight weeks, the lagging ones will follow. That is close to a physical law. If they're green and nothing is happening, the diagnostic below applies.

"It's not working" — a diagnostic

Work down this list in order. The answer is almost always in the first three rows.

CheckThe testThe fix
1. Has it been long enough?Fewer than 8 weeks of consistent training.Nothing is wrong. Keep going. Visible change starts around week 8–12.
2. Are you actually eating enough?Is your 7-day average weight higher than it was a month ago?If not, you are not in a surplus regardless of what you think you ate. Add 250 kcal/day and recheck in two weeks. Repeat until the scale moves.
3. Are the numbers going up in your log?Compare this week's sets to four weeks ago.If not, you're not progressively overloading. Check your rungs — you may be stuck on one too hard to add reps to. Regress a rung and rebuild.
4. Are you sleeping?Average under 7 hours.Fix this before changing anything about training. It is upstream of everything.
5. Is protein really at 150 g?Track carefully for three days.Most people who think they hit 150 g are at 90–110. Add a shake and one more protein-dense meal.
6. Are you training hard enough?Take one set to genuine failure. Were you actually 5+ reps away on your normal sets?Recalibrate. Working sets belong at 1–3 RIR.
7. Are you doing too much?More than ~20 hard sets per muscle per week, or adding extra work on rest days.Cut back to the program as written for four weeks.
8. Is the mirror lying?Compare month-1 and month-3 photos side by side, not from memory.This is the most common outcome: real change that you couldn't see because you see yourself every day.
