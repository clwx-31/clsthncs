Nutrition

You cannot build a body out of nothing

At 5'9" and 150 lb, undereating is the single most likely thing to stop you. Training is the signal that says "build muscle"; food is the raw material. Get both wrong and nothing happens. Get training right and food wrong, and still almost nothing happens.

Rate of gain — why slow wins

You can gain weight as fast as you like. You cannot gain muscle as fast as you like — muscle synthesis has a ceiling of roughly 1–2 lb per month for a well-trained, well-fed beginner. Every pound above that rate is fat.

RateSurplusRoughly what you getVerdict
+0.25 lb/wk~150 kcalNearly all muscle, very little fatExcellent if you're patient. Slow enough that a bad tracking week erases it.
+0.4 lb/wk~300 kcal~70% muscle, ~30% fatThe recommendation. Best ratio of results to fat gain for a beginner.
+0.75 lb/wk~550 kcal~50% muscle, ~50% fatAcceptable for a few months if you're very lean and impatient.
+1.5 lb/wk~1,000 kcal~25% muscle, ~75% fatA "dirty bulk." You end up 25 lb heavier, mostly fat, with worse pull-ups and a cut ahead of you.

Bodyweight matters more in calisthenics than in any other strength sport, because you are the load. Ten pounds of fat makes every pull-up, dip, handstand, and lever measurably harder — permanently, until you lose it. Ten pounds of muscle makes them easier. That's the whole argument for the slow lane.

How to steer. Every Sunday, average your seven morning weigh-ins. Compare to last Sunday's average. Gaining more than 0.5 lb/week? Cut 200 calories a day. Not gaining after three weeks? Add 250 calories a day. Adjust on three-week trends, never on a single day — daily weight swings 2–4 lb from water and food volume alone.

Protein — the number that matters most

Protein supplies the amino acids your body assembles into new muscle. It is also the most satiating and the most metabolically expensive macronutrient. If you get exactly one thing right in your diet, get this one.

SituationTargetFor you at 150 lb
Building muscle in a surplus0.7–1.0 g per lb105–150 g/day
Cutting (deficit)1.0–1.2 g per lb150–180 g/day
Absolute minimum to not lose ground0.5 g per lb75 g/day
Point of clearly diminishing returns>1.2 g per lb>180 g/day — no added benefit

Use 150 g as your daily target. That's 1 g per pound — comfortably above where the research shows benefits plateau, chosen because it's easy to remember and gives you margin for the days you undershoot.

Distribution matters more than timing

Muscle protein synthesis is stimulated by a dose of protein, and the response peaks around 0.25 g/lb (roughly 30–40 g for you) per meal. Beyond that, extra protein in one sitting mostly gets oxidized rather than doing more building. So:

  • Split across 4 meals of ~37 g each rather than 2 meals of 75 g.
  • Each meal should have an obvious protein source. If you look at a plate and can't point at the protein, it isn't there.
  • Include one before bed — 30–40 g of casein-rich protein (cottage cheese, Greek yogurt, milk, or a casein shake) supports overnight repair through a 7–9 hour fast.
  • The 30-minute post-workout "anabolic window" is a myth. The elevated response lasts many hours. Eat within a couple of hours and you've captured essentially all of it.
Plant-based? Aim about 20% higher (roughly 1.2 g/lb) because plant proteins are generally lower in leucine and less digestible, and combine sources across the day — legumes plus grains, soy, seitan, plus a pea/rice protein blend. Soy and pea protein isolate are the two plant proteins that hold up best in muscle-building research.

Carbohydrates — your training fuel

Carbs are stored in muscle as glycogen, which is the primary fuel for the kind of hard, repeated sets you're doing. Low-carb training feels flat, reduces the reps you can grind out, and makes recovery worse. They are also, for most people, the easiest way to actually hit a calorie surplus.

  • Target: 3–5 g per lb of bodyweight when bulking — 340–450 g at 150 lb. The calculator lands you around 375 g.
  • Base most of it on minimally processed sources: rice, oats, potatoes, pasta, bread, fruit, beans. These come with fiber and micronutrients.
  • Fast-digesting carbs around training (fruit, white rice, a bagel) are useful but not essential. Total daily intake matters far more.
  • Carbs are not fattening. A calorie surplus is fattening. Carbs are simply the most common source of one.

Fat — the one you shouldn't cut too low

Dietary fat is required for hormone production (including testosterone), absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K, and cell membrane structure. Very low-fat diets measurably lower testosterone, which is exactly backwards for someone trying to build muscle.

  • Target: 20–30% of calories, and never below 0.3 g per lb (45 g at 150 lb). The calculator gives you ~78 g.
  • Prioritize: olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds, fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), whole eggs.
  • Get omega-3s. Two servings of fatty fish per week, or a supplement. Anti-inflammatory, good for joints and recovery, and most people eat far too little.
  • Fat is calorie-dense (9 kcal/g vs 4 for protein and carbs), which makes it your best tool if you struggle to eat enough — see below.

Fiber, vegetables, and micronutrients

Not glamorous, but deficiencies here quietly wreck recovery, sleep, and energy.

  • Fiber: 25–35 g/day. Digestion, satiety, cholesterol, blood sugar. From beans, oats, fruit, vegetables, and whole grains.
  • Vegetables and fruit: aim for 5+ servings a day, and vary the colors — different pigments mean different micronutrients.
  • Vitamin D — most people in the US are low, especially in winter or with darker skin. Low vitamin D is linked to reduced strength and worse recovery. 1,000–2,000 IU/day is a cheap insurance policy.
  • Calcium (1,000 mg) and magnesium (400 mg) — muscle contraction, bone density, sleep quality. Dairy, leafy greens, nuts, seeds.
  • Iron and zinc — energy and immune function. Red meat, beans, shellfish. Vegetarians should pay attention here.
  • Sodium. Do not fear salt if you train and sweat. It matters for performance and blood volume. Just get most of it from food you cooked rather than from ultra-processed products.

Budget version (~$8/day)

Oats, eggs, and milk for breakfast. Lunch: rice, lentils, canned tuna, frozen vegetables. Snack: peanut butter sandwich. Dinner: chicken thighs (cheaper than breast), potatoes, frozen broccoli. Before bed: whole milk. Buy rice, oats, and beans in the largest bag available; buy chicken thighs and ground beef in family packs and freeze in portions; buy frozen vegetables, which are cheaper, last forever, and are nutritionally equivalent to fresh.

Vegetarian version

Breakfast: oats with milk, peanut butter, whey. Lunch: lentil and rice bowl with feta and olive oil. Snack: Greek yogurt with granola. Dinner: tofu or tempeh stir-fry with rice and edamame. Before bed: cottage cheese. Add a pea/rice protein blend to close the gap, and aim about 20% higher on total protein.

When you genuinely can't eat that much

If you have been 150 lb your whole life, 2,800 calories will feel like a lot at first. This is normal and it is a solvable engineering problem: reduce the volume of food, not the calories.

  • Drink calories. Whole milk, juice, and smoothies bypass fullness almost entirely. A shake of 8 oz milk + 1 cup oats + 2 tbsp peanut butter + 1 banana + 1 scoop whey ≈ 800 calories and 45 g protein in three minutes.
  • Add fat invisibly. A tablespoon of olive oil on rice, an extra spoon of peanut butter, cheese on everything. 100–150 calories at a time with no added bulk.
  • Choose the less filling version. White rice over brown, juice over whole fruit, dried fruit over fresh. Fiber is important, but you don't need it from every single source.
  • Eat more often, not more per sitting. Five meals of 560 calories is far easier than three of 930.
  • Don't fill up on vegetables at the start of a meal while bulking. Eat the calorie-dense food first, vegetables after.
  • Ramp up. Add 200 calories a week rather than jumping from 2,000 to 2,800 overnight. Appetite genuinely adapts within a few weeks — hunger hormones adjust.
  • Cut back on cardio if you're doing a lot. Every mile run is ~100 calories you now have to eat back.

How to track without going crazy

You don't need to weigh food forever. You need to do it accurately for a few weeks so that your eyeball estimates become good, then spot-check.

  1. Weeks 1–2: log everything in a food app (MyFitnessPal, Cronometer, MacroFactor, or a spreadsheet). Use a $12 kitchen scale. Weigh everything. This is tedious and it's a one-time education.
  2. Weeks 3–4: keep logging but estimate the small stuff. You'll now know that "a chicken breast" is 6 oz and a "handful of nuts" is 1 oz.
  3. After week 4: track protein and total calories only, and only on weekdays. Or build 4–5 standard meals you know the numbers for and rotate them.
  4. Forever: if the scale stops moving in the direction you want for three weeks, go back to strict tracking for one week to find out what drifted. It's almost always portion creep.
Two common tracking errors. First, forgetting to log cooking oil, sauces, and drinks — that's routinely 300–500 hidden calories a day. Second, logging weekdays but not weekends: two big social days can erase an entire week's deficit or surplus. Log the weekend too, at least at first.

Hydration

  • Baseline: about half your bodyweight in ounces — 75 oz (a bit over 2 quarts) at 150 lb. Add 16–24 oz per hour of training.
  • Check your urine. Pale straw is right. Clear means you're overdoing it; dark yellow means catch up.
  • Even 2% dehydration measurably reduces strength and endurance and makes training feel much harder than it should.
  • Coffee and tea count toward your total; the diuretic effect is far smaller than the water they contain.
  • Sports drinks are unnecessary for 60-minute sessions. Water and normal salty food cover it.

Meal timing and eating around workouts

Total daily intake accounts for the vast majority of the result. Timing is a small refinement, worth getting roughly right and not worth stressing about.

  • Pre-workout (1–3 hours before): a normal meal with carbs and protein. If you train early and can't stomach a meal, a banana and a scoop of whey 30 minutes out is plenty.
  • Don't train stuffed. A large meal within 30–60 minutes of training makes inverted rows, leg raises, and anything upside-down genuinely unpleasant.
  • Post-workout: eat within a couple of hours. Protein plus carbs. The urgency is far lower than the supplement industry implies.
  • Before bed: 30–40 g of slow protein. Cottage cheese, Greek yogurt, milk, or casein.
  • Training fasted is fine if you prefer it and it doesn't tank your performance — but there's no advantage to it when the goal is building muscle, and there is a modest downside in training quality.

Alcohol

Not a moral question — a mechanical one. Alcohol suppresses muscle protein synthesis for roughly 24 hours after a meaningful dose, badly disrupts deep sleep even when you fall asleep faster, contributes 7 empty calories per gram, and reduces the quality of the next day's training. A drink or two occasionally will not undo your progress. Regular heavy drinking will substantially slow it, mostly through the sleep pathway. If you drink, keep it away from training days and don't make it a habit on the night before a session.

Should you bulk or cut?

At 5'9" and 150 lb with a BMI of 22 and little muscle, this is an easy call: bulk. You are lean enough that you have plenty of room, and cutting from here would leave you skinny rather than defined — "skinny fat" is fixed by building muscle, never by losing more weight.

If you are…Do thisWhy
Under ~12% body fat (male) / ~22% (female)Lean bulkMaximum room to build before fat becomes a problem.
12–18% (male) / 22–28% (female)Lean bulk, or recomp at maintenanceBoth work. As a total beginner you can gain muscle and lose fat simultaneously at maintenance — a window that closes after the first year or so.
Above ~20% (male) / ~30% (female)Cut first, for 8–16 weeksNutrient partitioning is better when leaner, and lower bodyweight makes every calisthenics movement easier.

When to stop bulking: when your waist has grown more than about 2 inches, when you've reached roughly 18% body fat, or when pull-ups have clearly gotten harder from the added weight. Then run an 8–12 week cut at a 20% deficit with protein at 1.2 g/lb, keep training the same, and come back leaner and bigger than you started.
